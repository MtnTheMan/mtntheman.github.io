*Part 3 of the NHR Literature Series: Regional Guidebooks*

Ever since moving to Michigan, I have kept a copy of Stan Tekiela's *Trees of Michigan: Field Guide* within reach. It is the book I will crack open when I need to identify a tree, check where a species occurs within the state, or understand where I probably would not expect to find it. My research focuses more on management intentions, human dimensions, and the policy behind northern hardwood management than it does on silviculture by itself, but I still need enough baseline knowledge about trees to follow the decisions managers are making. For me, that knowledge often begins with a localized guidebook.

I used these kinds of books as often as I could during the Northern Hardwoods Megatrip. When I entered a new region, I would borrow a field guide, textbook, or whatever local reference somebody had available so I could better understand the forest composition and makeup around me. Many foresters had shelves of guidebooks in their offices. After a certain point in a career, a forester may not open them very often because the information is already "all up in the brain," but the books remain on the shelf as a foundational piece of literature that formed part of that person's education and understanding of the region.

These books form a third category of the literary ancestors behind the Northern Hardwoods Resilience Project's State of Management work. In [Part 1 of this literature series](https://www.mtntheman.com/northern-hardwoods/research/travel/2026/07/08/nhr-log-7-the-silviculture-textbooks-behind-nhr.html), I looked at the texts that carry formal management theory and practice from one generation of foresters to another. [Part 2](https://www.mtntheman.com/northern-hardwoods/research/travel/2026/08/19/nhr-log-23-the-narrative-books-behind-nhr.html) considered narrative books that help foresters, landowners, and the public understand active care and regional stewardship. Guidebooks usually begin one step earlier by teaching the scientific "what" of a forest: what is present, how to identify it, where it is expected to occur, and what the vegetation may tell us about the soils, disturbance history, moisture, and productive capacity of a site.

## From Identifying a Tree to Reading a Site

At their most basic level, guidebooks teach a forester how to identify the species within a forest. Tree-identification books still belong at the center of this literature. Before somebody can understand how to manage a species, they need to recognize it, distinguish it from the trees around it, and develop a sense of where it does and does not belong.

These books are often used outside the forestry profession as well. A landowner, student, naturalist, or anybody else with a curiosity about the forest where they live and work may begin with the same question: What kind of tree is this? From there, the questions can expand toward what makes up a forest and how the larger forest system works.

There is a difference, though, between a guide devoted strictly to tree identification and one that also teaches forest ecology or the larger working systems at play. A tree guide may help distinguish sugar maple from red maple through leaves, buds, bark, twigs, and branching patterns. An ecological guide may describe the plants growing underneath those trees, the soils beneath them, the disturbances that helped create the stand, and the combinations of species that indicate a particular kind of site.

That second kind of guide became increasingly interesting to me during the Megatrip. On several forest walks, foresters pointed out species of fern, flowers, and relatively rare understory plants that they used as indicators of site quality. These plants could be sensitive to disturbance, light conditions, invasive species pressure, moisture, or other aspects of the growing environment. If the conditions had aligned well enough for a sensitive species to persist, its presence could tell an experienced forester quite a bit about the site.

I have sometimes described that process as "almost pseudoscientific," although the phrase is probably harsher than what I mean. One presence-or-absence observation can compress a large number of contributing factors into something that feels like a check-the-box assessment. If the habitat is suitable enough for a particular indicator species, then the site may be "in the green, so to speak," with many of the conditions associated with a productive native forest already aligned. The plant does not explain every process operating there, but its presence can verify several things at once for a forester who knows what to look for.

A flora guide, habitat-type guide, or forest-community classification begins to put language around those relationships. It helps move the question from "What tree is this?" toward "What is this forest really made up of, and what are these species telling me about the site?"

## A Regional Way of Seeing

Foresters eventually develop a regional way of seeing. Within a single state or province, there may be smaller regions where soils, disturbance, tree growth, harvesting history, and local microclimates interact to produce different outcomes. A forester who has spent years working in one of those areas develops a sense of what is typical, what is unusual, and how a stand may respond to treatment.

A regional guidebook touches the surface of that knowledge by explaining what is present and what is not, the conditions under which a species or forest community would normally occur, and how those conditions change from one part of a state or province to another. Many state-based guides divide their political unit into forest zones, ecological regions, habitat types, or site classes. Those classifications may differ from the national systems or international ecological boundaries applied to the same land.

Ecology crosses political boundaries, while forestry education is still commonly organized within them. A book written in Michigan, Maine, Québec, or Nova Scotia carries some of the language through which foresters in that location were taught to comprehend the forest. The front matter may explain the state's forest regions, management history, or climatic divisions. The species descriptions tell readers what belongs there, where it grows, and how it has been understood within that local system.

These regional differences become especially visible around the fringe of the northern hardwood belt, where northern hardwoods blend into the Carolinian forest of southern Ontario, the Acadian forest of Maine and the Maritime provinces, the Allegheny hardwood systems of Pennsylvania, and the mixed forests of the Lake States. Books written in those places often treat northern hardwoods as one portion of a larger regional ecology. Comparing them gives us several regional ways of seeing, comprehending, and understanding what a forest is.

This regional shelf is intentionally mixed. Tree-identification books establish the species-level knowledge, while ecological guides explain how local foresters have been taught to interpret relationships among those species, their sites, and the surrounding region.

## The Lake States

Michigan is where I have the most personal experience with this kind of guide. [*Trees of Michigan: Field Guide*](https://blog.adventurepublications.net/2020/03/trees-of-michigan-a-field-guide-by-stan-tekiela/) by Stan Tekiela covers the state's tree species through photographs, range information, and identifying characteristics. It is accessible to somebody who is still learning the trees, which is exactly how I have used it since moving here.

Donald Dickmann's [*Michigan Forest Communities: A Field Guide and Reference*](https://www.canr.msu.edu/resources/michigan_forest_communities_-_a_field_guide_and_reference) starts with the trees but places them into 23 forest-community types. It describes the characteristic shrubs and herbs, the distribution of each community, its ecological history, and some of the management activities commonly applied there. It reaches past individual species and into the forest community as a working system, which is the ecological layer that a tree-identification guide generally leaves for other references.

Northern Wisconsin has an especially management-oriented example in John Kotar, Joseph Kovach, and Timothy Burger's [*A Guide to Forest Communities and Habitat Types of Northern Wisconsin*](https://dnr.wisconsin.gov/topic/forestmanagement/wfhtguide). Understory vegetation is used alongside soils, disturbance, and succession to characterize site potential. The guide gives formal names and field keys to the kinds of forest-floor observations that foresters were explaining to me during site visits.

Minnesota contributes both an identification reference and a community guide. Welby Smith's [*Trees and Shrubs of Minnesota*](https://www.upress.umn.edu/9780816640652/trees-and-shrubs-of-minnesota/) covers native and naturalized woody species while including their habitat, range, and ecology. The Minnesota Department of Natural Resources' [*Field Guide to the Native Plant Communities of Minnesota: Laurentian Mixed Forest Province*](https://www.dnr.state.mn.us/npc/classification.html) organizes northeastern and north-central Minnesota through characteristic vegetation, soils, landscape setting, and disturbance history.

Across these three states, many of the same northern hardwood species cross the political boundaries, but the books arrange them through somewhat different educational systems. Each state has built a language for explaining where its forest types occur and what managers should look for when they enter a stand.

## Ontario, Québec, and the Acadian Forest

The [*Field Guide to Forest Ecosystems of Central Ontario*](https://books.google.com/books/about/Field_Guide_to_Forest_Ecosystems_of_Cent.html?id=DGbQPQAACAAJ), developed by Brenda Chambers and colleagues for the Ontario Ministry of Natural Resources, organizes central Ontario forests through ecosites, vegetation types, and soil types. Its foundation in more than 1,500 field plots supports a field-scale system through which a manager can understand northern hardwoods where they meet boreal and Great Lakes forest types.

Québec's [*Petite flore forestière du Québec*](https://www.quebec.ca/agriculture-environnement-et-ressources-naturelles/forets/recherche-connaissances/publications) includes trees, shrubs, herbaceous plants, ferns, mosses, and lichens. The province's regional [ecological-type identification guides](https://www.quebec.ca/agriculture-environnement-et-ressources-naturelles/forets/recherche-connaissances/inventaire-forestier/types/ecologique) then use tree cover, understory indicator groups, physical setting, surface deposits, and potential vegetation to classify sites within particular ecological regions.

There is a clear progression in those Québec references from identifying an individual forest-floor plant to placing a stand within an ecological type and then locating that type within a regional classification used for planning and management. That progression resembles how site knowledge develops: first learning what is present, then learning what its presence means locally.

Nova Scotia's [*Trees of the Acadian Forest*](https://novascotia.ca/natr/forestry/TreeID/) retains the tree-identification side of the shelf and places those species within the Acadian forest region. The province's [Forest Ecosystem Classification](https://novascotia.ca/natr/forestry/programs/ecosystems/ecosystems.asp) is divided into guides for vegetation types, soil types, and ecosites. Its [vegetation-type guide](https://novascotia.ca/natr/forestry/veg-types/introduction.asp) uses overstory and ground vegetation together to describe stand-level conditions, which gives the reader a localized way of interpreting an Acadian forest through more than the canopy.

## New England and the Southern Edge

Michael Wojtech's [*Bark: A Field Guide to Trees of the Northeast*](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/distributed/B/bo69891437.html) was one of the first books I thought of for this collection. It teaches identification through bark, which remains visible through every season and becomes increasingly useful as a forester gains experience. Its coverage across much of the Northeast also makes it a useful contrast to the state-based guides.

The Maine Forest Service's [*Forest Trees of Maine*](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mfs/publications/handbooks_guides/forest_trees/index.html) supplies the state-level identification layer, including summer and winter keys. Susan Gawler and Andrew Cutko's [*Natural Landscapes of Maine: A Guide to Natural Communities and Ecosystems*](https://www.maine.gov/dacf/mnap/publications/community_classification.htm) moves into the relationships among trees, shrubs, wildflowers, soils, landscape settings, and the state's natural communities. Maine is a particularly useful example because northern hardwoods occur within the larger Acadian system, and the guides have to explain that overlap.

Farther south, Donald Leopold's [*Trees of New York State: Native and Naturalized*](https://press.syr.edu/supressbooks/1126/trees-of-new-york-state/) combines identification keys with range, habitat, and natural-history information. New York's [*Ecological Communities of New York State*](https://gisservices.dec.ny.gov/gis/erm/naturalHeritageCommunities.html) places those species within the community classification used by the New York Natural Heritage Program.

Pennsylvania follows a similar path through Ann Fowler Rhoads and Timothy Block's *Trees of Pennsylvania: A Complete Reference Guide* and the Pennsylvania Natural Heritage Program's [plant-community classification](https://naturalheritage.dcnr.pa.gov/Communities.aspx). The latter describes terrestrial and palustrine communities using vegetation, soils, geology, distribution, and conservation context. These references become especially useful where northern hardwoods meet Allegheny and Appalachian forest systems.

The New Jersey Forest Service's [*Trees of New Jersey and the Mid-Atlantic States*](https://dep.nj.gov/parksandforests/conservation/tree-books/) offers the identification layer for New Jersey and the surrounding region. Beryl Robichaud Collins's [*Plant Communities of New Jersey: A Study in Landscape Diversity*](https://www.rutgersuniversitypress.org/plant-communities-of-new-jersey/9780813520711) relates the state's vegetation to geology, topography, soils, climate, and human disturbance. By the time northern hardwoods reach this part of the study region, they are one part of a much larger transition among northern, Appalachian, oak-dominated, and coastal forest communities.

## Every Stand Is Different

There is a concept that I cannot state enough in these logs, which is that every stand is different. It is easier to say than to really show, but every stand of trees has a different shape, harvesting history, current species composition, soils, microclimates, and set of conditions acting on it. Even within a relatively small region and a similar climatic regime, two stands can respond differently to the same treatment.

A manager learns those differences by spending time with the land, watching how trees respond to harvest, seeing which species recruit under different light conditions, remembering how quickly they grow, and recognizing which treatments worked and which did not. It would be a fool's errand to try to put all of that into one book. Several managers have described spending an entire career learning a region and finally feeling that they have a handle on it when they have about five years left in their career.

[Narrative texts](https://www.mtntheman.com/northern-hardwoods/research/travel/2026/08/19/nhr-log-23-the-narrative-books-behind-nhr.html) can convey some of that intrinsic localized knowledge, while [planning documents](https://www.mtntheman.com/northern-hardwoods/research/travel/2026/08/21/nhr-log-24-variability-in-planning-documentation.html) can preserve pieces of what somebody intended to do, what treatment was implemented, and sometimes what happened afterward. Apprenticeship offers another route for carrying that understanding across generations of managers. Guidebooks operate near the beginning of the learning process by giving somebody enough vocabulary to recognize what they are seeing and ask better questions about it.

The guidebook is kind of the first step in bridging that gap. Baseline curiosity leads to identifying a species, recognizing the plants associated with it, understanding something about the site where it grows, and eventually learning how that species responds to management. Years of experience still have to fill in the rest.

For the Northern Hardwoods Resilience Project, this shelf helps explain how people across the region have been taught to comprehend a working forest at the local level. A range-wide assessment brings several of those regional ways of seeing into the same project, even though the names, classifications, and cues used in each place may differ. The regional shelf is not exhaustive and can be iteratively improved as we encounter other tree guides, field manuals, flora references, and ecological classifications that have influenced northern hardwood management.
