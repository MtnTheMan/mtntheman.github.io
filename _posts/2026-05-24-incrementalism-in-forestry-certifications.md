*Thinking through regional credential systems across the Northern Hardwoods*

One thing that has come up in conversations on this trip is how differently forestry credentials are built from place to place. That sounds obvious, but it starts to matter once you compare systems across the northern hardwood range. Some jurisdictions use full professional licensure. Some rely more on voluntary standards, markets, and professional reputation. Ontario has another layer: targeted credentials for specific forestry tasks, especially tree marking on Crown land.

That Ontario example is what pushed me toward the idea of incrementalism here. By incrementalism, I mean a system that adds credentials around particular tasks instead of treating professional forestry as a single all-or-nothing license. A person may be certified to mark trees, write certain timber management plans, or do specialized fieldwork inside a defined institutional setting without carrying the full professional license that another jurisdiction might require. That creates a middle category between voluntary professional norms and full licensure (Canadian Institute of Forestry; Maine Board of Licensure of Foresters).

## The spectrum of professional licensure

At one end are states and provinces that regulate professional forestry tightly. In Maine and New Hampshire, individuals cannot legally provide professional forestry services for compensation without a state license (University of New Hampshire Extension; Maine Board of Licensure of Foresters). Maine requires applicants to hold a forestry degree accredited by the Society of American Foresters, complete an internship, pass a national exam, and keep up with continuing education (Maine Board of Licensure of Foresters). Canadian provinces such as Ontario and Québec also have professional orders that govern practice across public and private forestlands (Légis Québec; Canadian Institute of Forestry). The logic is straightforward: before someone can sell professional forestry services, the state or province makes them meet a general professional standard.

At the other end are places that leave much more to the market and to professional norms. Vermont, New York, and Pennsylvania do not maintain state licensure for consulting foresters, which means a person can do timber cruising or harvest marking without first passing through a state licensing board (VTDigger, 2016). Wisconsin takes a similar path on mandatory licensing, while offering a voluntary Certificate of Service for tracking education. This kind of open system lowers barriers to entry and gives landowners more options, but it also shifts the burden of judging competence onto the landowner (VTDigger, 2016).

## Modular credentials and the Ontario case

Ontario's Tree Marker Training Program sits between those approaches. The program began in 1995 and requires participants to complete multi-day training that pairs silvicultural instruction with field-based tree selection exercises (Canadian Institute of Forestry; Wilson, Edward R, 2015). Level 1 candidates need at least 80 percent on written exams and 70 percent in field assessments. The certificate lasts five years, and renewal requires retraining (Wilson, Edward R, 2015). On Crown land, tree marking is not just a skill someone claims to have. It is a task tied to a required certificate (Canadian Institute of Forestry).

That kind of task-level credential is not common outside Ontario. Across the northeastern United States and the Great Lakes region, state agencies generally do not offer independent tree-marking credentials. Tree selection is usually treated as part of a forester's broader, degree-based responsibilities (Canadian Institute of Forestry). In places without local programs, some practitioners use outside credentials to signal competence. Consultants in Nova Scotia have used Ontario certifications to distinguish their private woodlot work (Butternut Consulting), and some U.S. consultants pursue SAF Certified Forester credentials even when their state does not require them. Michigan offers another hybrid case: the state does not have general forester licensing, but its Qualified Forest Program requires a registered Qualified Forester to author timber management plans for private property tax incentives (Michigan Department of Agriculture and Rural Development). That controls quality at a specific policy doorway without licensing the whole field.

Licensure is not the only way standards reach the woods. Voluntary certification programs shape behavior too, just through different tools. The American Tree Farm System includes about 74,000 family forests and roughly 20 million acres nationwide, using management plans, recognition, and market incentives rather than legal permission (Alabama Forestry Foundation). Enrolled landowners receive documented management plans and program signage, and in some supply chains certified timber can matter to mills (Canadian Institute of Forestry). State Forest Stewardship programs and international systems such as the Forest Stewardship Council work in a similar policy space, where the standard travels through access, recognition, and market preference more than through a license.

That matters because much of northern hardwood governance is not only a question of law. It is also a question of who has the time, money, training, and institutional support to do careful work. The useful question is where each model fits. Targeted credentials can make sense when a task is technical, repeated, and important enough to deserve a shared standard. A full license can make more sense when the public needs one accountable professional standard across many services. Voluntary systems fit somewhere else again, especially where regulation is politically difficult or where landowners enter through markets and incentive programs instead of permits.

None of those choices are neutral. A light regulatory system may keep people working but leave landowners responsible for judging competence. A heavy system may protect standards but add barriers and paperwork. A modular system may build useful pathways for technicians and field specialists, but it can also split oversight across several credentials and agencies. The local forest economy, enforcement capacity, landowner base, and public ownership pattern decide how much each tradeoff matters.

That is the policy thread I want to keep following. Northern hardwoods cross states and provinces, but the people managing them work inside particular legal and professional systems. If the NHR project is going to compare management across the range, those systems belong in the explanation of how practice gets made on the ground.
