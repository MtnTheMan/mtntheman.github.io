*Part 2 of the NHR Literature Series: Narrative Texts*

During interviews for the Northern Hardwoods Resilience Project, conversations that began with regeneration, prescriptions, markets, or land ownership would sometimes end up somewhere less technical: foresters trying to explain their work to people who do not read silviculture textbooks and are probably never going to. Many foresters reflected on the fact that their management was difficult when they did not feel they had the [social license](https://www.mtntheman.com/northern-hardwoods/research/travel/2026/08/12/nhr-log-20-the-social-license-to-cut.html) to practice forestry at the level a stand required, whether that meant a treatment aggressive enough to change a degraded system or something softer because that was what made ecological and silvicultural sense in that particular place. When people had an answer for how foresters might communicate across that gap, narrative books came up over a dozen times in the interviews. They were described as a first front line of communication with landowners and, through them, with the public. A field guide can help someone identify a plant, a textbook can explain a silvicultural system, and a scientific paper can supply part of the technical support backbone. Those sources rarely guide the human dimension of understanding and social capital in the same way that a story can.

I have been thinking of these books as one category in a larger set of spiritual ancestors for the Northern Hardwoods Resilience Project. They are the non-textbook books: memoirs, essays, narrative nonfiction, and even a novel that give us vignettes of varying depth about what it feels like to care for, work in, inherit, alter, and argue over a forest. The [silviculture textbooks behind NHR](https://www.mtntheman.com/northern-hardwoods/research/travel/2026/07/08/nhr-log-7-the-silviculture-textbooks-behind-nhr.html) formed the first shelf in this series. Their value for the State of Management work is partly in the forestry they describe, but it is also in the language they make available to foresters and readers who are trying to decide what responsible management could look like.

I do not think it is fair to expect these books to reach the broadest public. It is very rare to hear a radio ad about responsible forestry, and most people are probably not looking for a book about northern hardwoods unless they already have a deeper interest in forests. The audience may instead be a forest landowner who inherited land without formal forestry education, does not yet know where to find a consulting forester, or has simply never had the woods at the front of their day-to-day decision making. A paperback, a Kindle download, or an audiobook can become one of that person's first information streams. Later, what they repeat to a neighbor, family member, logger, or forester becomes a kind of secondary evidence of the book's reach.

That is a narrower path to the public than broad mass communication, but it is a consequential one for northern hardwoods. Private owners hold a large portion of the resource, including well over half on the U.S. side and perhaps just under half in Canada once Crown land is accounted for. Across that land base, active management depends on people who have different levels of knowledge, different relationships to their property, and different ideas about what caring for a forest means. These books give us a starting place for understanding what some of those landowners may know before a forester ever arrives.

## *How to Love a Forest* and the work of active care

Ethan Tapper's *How to Love a Forest: The Bittersweet Work of Tending a Changing World* deserves the longest treatment in this collection because it most directly takes on the tension foresters kept describing. Tapper writes as a forester who wants to share his experience with the world, including the uncomfortable parts of active management, and the world can respond in kind by accepting, questioning, or rejecting that viewpoint. He is writing about love for forests without treating love as a reason to avoid every difficult intervention.

The book follows Tapper's work in a forest shaped by past use and continuing pressures, and it asks what responsibility looks like once a person has decided to act. Cutting trees, controlling invasive plants, responding to deer browse, and making choices whose results may take decades to see can all look destructive when separated from the condition of the stand and the intention behind the work. Tapper keeps that context attached. He explains why care can require disturbance, effort, and the willingness to produce benefits for humans while remaining cognizant of conservation details. That combination helps explain why the book came up so often. These narrative texts are probably the most enjoyable way for someone to get familiar with forestry, and *How to Love a Forest* gives a landowner a window into active forest management without asking that person to begin with verbose academic language. Within its regional bounds, the book shows how a forester thinks through action when leaving a degraded stand alone would also be a choice with consequences.

Its reach also seemed regional in the interviews. People referred to Tapper outside Vermont and New Hampshire, but the book was clearly most present in conversations there, where his work and public voice are grounded. I take that as an interview observation rather than a claim about sales. Still, it makes me wonder what else could become regionally influential if northern hardwood management had regional champions across the full range. I do not think we have an Ethan Tapper for Michigan. We have people who care about forests, advocates, and professionals who talk about northern hardwood management, markets, and responsible practice, but I cannot immediately point to someone who brings every local part of that context together with the same artistic and narrative flow. The gap is more specific than a shortage of conservation writing. Arguments for preserving old growth or protecting an endangered species can be compelling, but on their face they can also end with leaving a place alone. A northern hardwood manager often has to explain why proactive work, including work that looks messy or forceful in the short term, may improve conditions in a stand that is already degraded.

Any regional champion would have to build their own experience and perspective into that explanation. Northern hardwood management is entirely region dependent: climates are different, cultures are different, markets are different, and policies change what is feasible and what leverage a manager has to push northern hardwood health in the right direction. Being cognizant of the whole system means something different in Michigan than it does in Vermont. A Lake States voice should sound like the Lake States, including the ownership patterns, industrial history, tribal stewardship, deer pressure, mill infrastructure, and local expectations that shape what a forester can actually do.

## A regional forest told at a regional scale

David Dobbs and Richard Ober's *The Northern Forest* approaches the communication problem from farther back. Rather than centering one forester's decisions, it gathers stories about the forests, communities, economies, ownership changes, and conservation debates of northern New England and New York. The result is a regional portrait of a working forest whose future cannot be separated from the people living in and around it. For NHR, the regional scale keeps a stand-level prescription inside the ownership and community histories that surround it. A prescription can be technically sound while the larger land base is being divided, sold, consolidated, conserved, or managed under a completely different set of expectations. By moving among people and places, Dobbs and Ober show how the meaning of the northern forest changes depending on who is speaking: a landowner, a logger, a conservationist, a town, or someone thinking about the region from outside it.

The book also gives us an older public-facing account to compare with newer ones. Its view of the future was assembled roughly three decades ago, so some of its immediate circumstances have changed while many of its tensions remain familiar. It records what responsible use, conservation, and regional continuity could be made to mean at that time, before readers bring today's language about resilience, climate adaptation, carbon, and changing wood markets to the same forest.

Tapper's work and *The Northern Forest* therefore do different jobs. One brings the reader close to the choices involved in tending a particular forest; the other shows why those choices sit inside a larger social and regional arrangement. Together they also reveal a limitation in this shelf so far: the Northeast has recognizable narrative treatments of northern hardwood management, while comparable Lake States accounts are harder to name.

## Broader books that have shaped the language of care

Some books belong here even though northern hardwood forestry is not their central subject. *Braiding Sweetgrass* by Robin Wall Kimmerer brings Indigenous knowledge, plant ecology, reciprocity, gratitude, and responsibility into the same conversation. Many readers who would never open a silviculture text have encountered forests and plants through Kimmerer's language, and that language can shape what they expect from stewardship before they meet a forester or make a decision about their own land. I do not need a detailed summary of *Braiding Sweetgrass* here, because its broad reach and central ideas are already familiar to many readers. For this project, I am more interested in what happens when its language of reciprocal care meets the operational choices of forestry. Reciprocity does not hand a manager a prescription, set a residual basal area, or determine when a harvest should occur. It changes the questions a person may ask about obligation, taking, renewal, and relationship, which then become part of the human setting in which technical decisions are explained.

*A Sand County Almanac* fills a different place, especially for the Lake States. Aldo Leopold's essays grew from a Wisconsin landscape and left an enduring impression on conservation across the region through the land ethic: a way of treating soils, waters, plants, animals, and people as members of a connected community. The book is a classic rather than a recent account of northern hardwood management, and I would keep its treatment brief for that reason. Its presence gives the Lake States a historical and ethical anchor, even if it does not fill the current gap represented by the sentence, "I don't think we have an Ethan Tapper for Michigan." Leopold helped shape how generations of conservationists and resource professionals understood their relationship to land. A present-day champion has to work inside the forest conditions, public expectations, ownership arrangements, and policy constraints that exist now, then explain what active northern hardwood management looks like under those conditions. An influential regional ancestor and a contemporary regional champion leave different kinds of work behind for readers.

Daniel Mason's novel *North Woods* adds another route into the human dimension. The book stays with one place as different people occupy it across centuries, allowing inheritance, attachment, disturbance, succession, use, and loss to accumulate on the same ground. Its forestry contribution is indirect, but the long view fits a resource where land can outlast several owners and where the consequences of a decision may be inherited by someone who had no part in making it. That is close to one of the practical problems raised in the interviews. Many people inherit forestland with little formal knowledge of how to care for it, and the property may arrive with past harvests, deferred decisions, family stories, financial needs, and expectations that are difficult to separate. *North Woods* does not tell that person what treatment to choose. It gives emotional and narrative weight to the fact that ownership is temporary even when an owner's choices remain on the land.

## Other books on the shelf

Three additional books can remain on the shelf without receiving full summary treatment. Stephen Long's *Thirty-Eight: The Hurricane That Transformed New England* follows the 1938 hurricane and the way a major disturbance altered forests, communities, and later decisions across New England. It offers another narrative route into the relationship between disturbance and management, although its regional center again falls in the Northeast. Mary Siisip Geniusz's *Plants Have So Much to Give Us, All We Have to Do Is Ask*, edited by Wendy Makoons Geniusz and illustrated by Annmarie Geniusz, brings Anishinaabe plant knowledge and relationships into a Lake States context. I originally considered giving it a larger place, but within this smaller collection it overlaps some of the ground already represented by *Braiding Sweetgrass*. It belongs in the honorable mentions precisely because the regional perspective is relevant and the book should remain visible without forcing two short summaries to repeat one another.

Suzanne Simard's *Finding the Mother Tree* also belongs among the influential general forest books. It has given a broad readership a memorable account of forest connectivity and of Simard's life in forestry, but it comes from western conifer systems rather than the northern hardwood resource. It has also drawn debate over how scientific findings are translated into a public narrative and how far readers should generalize them. If it receives more space in a later version of this post, I would want to treat that tension directly rather than presenting the book's influence as the same thing as technical agreement. This list will remain incomplete. There are certainly other regional books, memoirs, novels, and essay collections that landowners or foresters use when they talk about responsible management, and I would rather build the shelf iteratively than pretend the first version captures everything. The missing Lake States examples are especially worth finding. If a book has shaped how people in Michigan, Wisconsin, Minnesota, Ontario, or elsewhere in the northern hardwood range understand active management, I want to know what it is and where it has had that influence.

## What narrative texts contribute to the State of Management

These books cannot tell us whether a particular treatment regenerated sugar maple, met a structural target, or produced the intended conditions after an entry. That work belongs to other evidence streams in NHR: management plans and records, interviews, on-the-ground inventories, textbooks, field guides, and scientific papers. Narrative texts tell us something adjacent that those sources often leave thin. They show how forestry has been communicated to people who may own forests, vote on forest policy, live beside managed lands, hire foresters, oppose harvests, or try to decide what responsible care means without technical training. They also provide a baseline knowledge of how far forestry is communicating with the public. The baseline is uneven. Someone who has read Tapper, Kimmerer, Leopold, Simard, or one of the other books on this shelf may enter a forestry conversation with a developed moral vocabulary and still know very little about the specific ecology or silviculture of their stand. Someone who has read none of them may understand a forest through family history, recreation, local employment, visual expectations, or a general belief that caring for woods means leaving them alone. Those are different starting places for the same management conversation.

For the State of Management work, I want to understand those starting places alongside the decisions that follow. Foresters are working with the condition of the land, but they are also working with the information streams and cultural expectations surrounding that land. If a manager lacks the social license to use the treatment that makes sense, the barrier is part of the management context. If a narrative book helps a landowner see why active care can include cutting, disturbance, patience, and uncertainty, that book has entered the system even though it will never appear in a prescription.

The regional gaps may tell us as much as the books themselves. Vermont and New Hampshire have a recent, recognizable example of a forester writing publicly across technical and emotional dimensions. The Lake States have an enormous conservation inheritance through Leopold and strong regional writing about plants, land, and ecology, yet I still struggle to name a current narrative champion who gathers northern hardwood management, markets, culture, and local practice into one public-facing voice. Maybe that person or book already exists and I have missed it. Maybe the communication is happening through smaller campaigns, local organizations, extension work, newsletters, or conversations that never become a mass-market book. Either way, the absence is part of what I want this literature series to record. The State of Management work also has to ask who is explaining forestry, who is listening, which stories travel, and which regions have language that people can carry from a book into a decision about their woods. Textbooks and papers support the technical backbone of the project. These narrative texts help us understand the people expected to live with, authorize, and sometimes undertake the work.
