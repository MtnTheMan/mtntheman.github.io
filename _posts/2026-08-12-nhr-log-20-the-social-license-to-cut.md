Across the Northern Hardwoods interview road trip, from the easternmost terminus to the westernmost end of the northern hardwood forest, I kept hearing foresters describe treatments that made silvicultural sense and then, somewhere in the explanation, shift into what they felt the public would actually allow them to do. They might have the legal authority to manage a stand and a prescription that they could defend scientifically, but that did not necessarily mean they felt comfortable opening the canopy as much as the desired species required. The missing piece was often some version of social license.

I already used that term in [NHR Log 9: Do We All Remember the Lorax Properly?](https://www.mtntheman.com/northern-hardwoods/research/travel/2026/07/13/nhr-log-9-do-we-all-remember-the-lorax-properly.html), which was mainly about the public story of forestry and how responsible management competes with the much simpler image of cutting as destruction. I want this post to take on a narrower question: what does social license actually mean in forestry, and how does it affect the range of treatments a forester feels able to propose?

## A license without a licensing office

A social license to operate is different from a legal license, permit, management plan, or timber-sale contract. There is no application for it and no one moment when an agency or forester can say that they have secured it permanently. The term describes the continuing acceptance and legitimacy that communities and other interested publics give to an activity beyond whatever formal authority already exists. In the literature, that acceptance is generally connected to trust in the people doing the work, whether the decision process was fair, whether the organization has responded to local concerns, and whether the outcomes seem consistent with what people were told would happen ([Moffat et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1093/forestry/cpv044)).

I think social capital is related to that process, although I do not think the terms are interchangeable. I have been using social capital to describe the trust, relationships, familiarity, and shared understanding that get built up before a difficult treatment is proposed. Social license is closer to the room a forester has to act once that foundation exists.

You could say that foresters build social capital and then spend it on treatments, which is close to the way some people on the road described the situation. The metaphor gets a little strange if it is taken too literally, since trust does not work like a bank account. A forester can be highly respected locally and still encounter opposition to one particular harvest, while an agency can be distrusted broadly even though the person marking the stand has spent years building relationships with the people who actually use that forest.

The U.S. Forest Service literature often approaches the same problem through the phrase *social acceptability*. Shindler, Brunson, and Stankey described public judgments about management as provisional, with each practice and setting producing its own circumstances rather than leading toward a final state of acceptance that an agency can reach and then retain ([Shindler, Brunson, and Stankey 2002](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/3242)). Ribe later separated “broad-trust acceptability,” or confidence in an agency and its general direction, from the public response to a particular project in a particular place ([Ribe 2013](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/44815)).

That separation made sense after listening to foresters talk across the range. Someone may support forestry, local wood products, wildlife habitat, and active forest management in a general sense, then oppose the harvest beside the trail they walk every week. Another person may distrust an agency as a whole and still believe the local forester who has answered questions for twenty years and can explain what happened in a stand ten or fifteen years after it was cut.

The Canadian literature adds another scale to this. Sen Wang first described forest management as operating under a new social contract and later organized social license as a nested arrangement of local consent, citizen endorsement, and broader societal support ([Wang 2005](https://doi.org/10.5558/tfc81486-4); [Wang 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.forpol.2019.05.006)). Dare, Schirmer, and Vanclay similarly found that forestry organizations operate with multiple social licenses across local, regional, and societal settings, which can be weakened when public engagement reaches a narrow group of people or when the organization has very little capacity to respond to what it hears ([Dare, Schirmer, and Vanclay 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/14615517.2014.927108)).

Stephen Wyatt’s work in Canada also keeps the concept from becoming too simple. Aboriginal rights, territorial relationships, and political authority cannot be reduced to another form of stakeholder approval, and a social-license framework can hide those distinctions if every affected group is treated as part of one undifferentiated public ([Wyatt 2016](https://doi.org/10.1093/forestry/cpw034)). So the question is never simply whether *the public* accepts forestry. There are different publics, different levels of authority, and very different histories behind the relationship each group has with a forest.

## What an aggressive cut is trying to grow

Yellow birch kept bringing this conversation out into the open during the interviews. Yellow birch is generally described as intermediate in shade tolerance, and its regeneration benefits from canopy openings along with disturbed humus or mixed mineral-soil seedbeds. It is more tolerant than the other native birches, but less tolerant than sugar maple and beech, and it generally cannot regenerate under a closed canopy ([USDA Forest Service, “Yellow Birch”](https://www.srs.fs.usda.gov/pubs/misc/ag_654/volume_2/betula/alleghaniensis.htm)).

Aspen creates an even clearer example. Aspen is very shade-intolerant and often responds to substantial canopy removal by producing abundant root suckers, which means that a clearcut can be the silviculturally appropriate treatment when maintaining an aspen stand is actually the objective ([USDA Forest Service, “Quaking Aspen”](https://research.fs.usda.gov/silvics/quaking-aspen)). A clearcut would often be the wrong approach in the classical sugar maple and beech systems that people tend to picture when they hear “northern hardwoods,” but yellow birch, white birch, and aspen all complicate the idea that removing fewer trees is automatically the more ecologically responsible choice.

For species that need light, an overly conservative treatment can undermine the regeneration objective before the new cohort has much of a chance. The forester may be looking at how much light reaches the ground, whether there is a receptive seedbed, which seed sources remain, what advance regeneration is already present, and what will happen when sugar maple or beech gets a head start. Someone walking past the job may reasonably begin with the more immediate observation that a lot of trees are gone.

That difference in how a treatment is read is where the word *aggressive* becomes difficult. An opening can look aggressive from the trail because the slash is visible and the remaining trees no longer create the enclosed forest experience people remember. From the forester’s perspective, the treatment may be a fairly specific attempt to create enough light, retain selected legacy trees, and move a stand toward a more diverse composition.

Several foresters described being softer about certain treatments because of the public response they expected. They might reduce the size of an opening, leave more canopy, avoid a highly visible location, or decide that the administrative and political fight attached to a treatment would be greater than the organization could absorb. Some of those adjustments are reasonable. Public forests have to accommodate recreation, aesthetics, habitat, local history, and other values that do not disappear because a prescription is silviculturally defensible.

The problem shows up when the visually acceptable treatment and the treatment needed to regenerate the desired species move too far apart. At that point, a forester can end up choosing between what the public will tolerate and what seems most likely to produce the stand described in the management objectives. If that pattern happens repeatedly, public perception begins shaping species composition through all of the prescriptions that never get written or are softened before implementation.

Public reluctance also has a history. There are bad forest actors, and irresponsible active management can put a forest on a worse trajectory. High grading removes the best and most valuable stems while leaving poorer-quality growing stock behind, and repeated entries of that kind can narrow what the next forester has available to work with. In a stand already dominated by beech in the understory and overstory, removing the valuable stems while leaving beech to keep expanding can contribute to the dense beech thickets that managers are now confronting alongside beech bark disease and beech leaf disease.

Forestry cannot build public trust by treating all skepticism as ignorance or by asking people to assume that every harvest is responsible. The distinction between responsible and irresponsible management has to appear in what was retained, what regenerates, whether the stated objectives are being met, and whether anyone comes back after the equipment leaves. Public trust grows when a treatment can be connected to the future forest with more than a sign at the landing and an assurance that the forester knows best.

## Fifteen years after the harvest

I think we need to do a better job of telling the story of a harvest fifteen years after the harvest. Most people encounter a job when it is at its visually roughest, with fresh stumps, slash, open ground, disturbed soil, and an understory that may look more like a wall of shrubs than a future forest. The forester may be thinking several entries ahead, while the person walking the trail is responding to what happened beside it last winter.

Fifteen years is not a crazy amount of time in the grand scheme of being a landowner, and it is an especially short period in the management of public land. It can still be long enough for the logic of a regeneration treatment to become much easier to see.

I remember walking through a stand in the Northeast Kingdom of Vermont that had been harvested fifteen years earlier. I was really fighting my way through a whole bunch of pin cherry and bushes while talking with the forester who had prepared the job. They had not been back to that stand in a little while, and as we pushed our way through it, they were seeing how much regeneration had established in what I would call the extended aftermath of the harvest.

The transcript from that interview is probably half annotations saying “muffled leaves,” because we were constantly moving through all of that vegetation. Underneath the noise, there was abundant regeneration working its way up and through. The trees retained for diversity or legacy were still there, and the composition of the next stand was becoming visible if you knew what you were looking at.

A well-used trail ran directly through the harvest, so the forestry practice itself was visible in a way that it often is not. There was regeneration, there were clear objectives and outcomes that the forester could point toward, and the site remained a very high-use public area. The forester had been required to consider all of the different needs and demands that public foresters are usually subject to, and the stand gave us a place to talk about how those decisions had worked out over time.

They were very proud of their work, as they should have been, and I was honestly a little proud of their work too. I could see the vision. It was a very good representation of what forestry can look like when the regeneration, retained structure, public use, and health of a stand can be discussed through several different measures instead of judging the job entirely by how it looked immediately after cutting.

That kind of experience also helps explain why a brief informational campaign can have limited effects. In the Indiana Hardwood Ecosystem Experiment, a short explanation of management practices produced a statistically significant increase in acceptability, but the practical change in attitudes was small, and both recreationists and neighboring landowners generally preferred denser stands and less open treatments ([Rogers, Hoover, and Allred 2013](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/42926)). A study of pine barrens restoration in northeastern Wisconsin found a similar decline in acceptance as treatments became more intensive and created more openness, although providing information improved the reception of some treatments ([Arnberger et al. 2022](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/64376)).

A sign or short explanation can describe the prescription, but it cannot show fifteen years of forest development all at once. Repeated photographs, field walks, regeneration measurements, and later visits can give people a way to see whether the treatment produced what the forester said it would produce. And if it did not, that follow-up gives the forester a way to say what failed, what changed, and what they would do differently the next time.

That is a more demanding form of communication than installing a sign while the job is active. It asks the organization to remain attached to its own decisions and to make the later stand part of the public story. I think those fifteen-year examples could do far more for forestry’s social license than another abstract defense of active management, especially when the public can walk the site and see the regeneration for themselves.

## Disturbance, chablis, and the art of forestry

There is also a simple linguistic part of this that I keep coming back to. In forestry, disturbance is a necessary component of producing outcomes in almost any stand, whether it comes through fire, wind, ice, insects, soil disturbance, individual tree mortality, or a larger event that changes the amount of light reaching the forest floor. Natural disturbance has always been a part of these systems.

In Quebec forestry language, there is a word that I like for this: *chablis*. Strictly speaking, *chablis* refers to a tree or group of trees that has been overturned, uprooted, or broken by a natural cause, often wind or snow ([Office québécois de la langue française](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/fiche-gdt/fiche/8872017/chablis)). Quebec forestry research refers to partial *chablis* among the natural disturbance patterns that irregular shelterwood systems can try to reproduce by creating canopy openings while retaining trees of different sizes and ages ([Québec Ministère des Ressources naturelles et des Forêts](https://mrnf.gouv.qc.ca/nos-publications/thuya-epinette-rouge-oupe-progressive-irreguliere-plantation-enrichissement/)).

The word has stayed with me because deadwood is simply part of having a forest. There have always been storms, heavy winds, individual treefalls, and places where one tree or one group of trees comes down and changes the conditions underneath it. A forest that appears undisturbed is usually being observed during one period between disturbance events.

Forestry causes disturbance for human purposes and then attempts to regenerate the forest that follows. A good forester is reading the species, soils, existing regeneration, stand history, markets, and public expectations, then trying to decide what type and amount of disturbance can move that particular stand in a better direction. A harvest can imitate some conditions produced by natural disturbance without pretending that a logging operation and a windstorm are the same event.

That is where I see the art form alongside the science. The science helps describe how much light yellow birch needs, how aspen responds after canopy removal, how residual trees react to exposure, and how regeneration changes under deer pressure or competing vegetation. The art is in reading a particular stand and assembling those pieces into a treatment that can work ecologically, economically, operationally, and socially. Forestry done well should feel scientific and artistic at the same time.

## What social license allows a forester to do

Silviculture defines the treatments that could work, legal authority defines what a forester is formally allowed to do, and social license influences what that forester feels able to propose and defend. When every large opening is expected to become a political fight, the range of treatments gradually narrows. That narrowing can influence regeneration and future species composition long before the effects are obvious to anyone outside the profession.

Foresters need enough public trust to use the treatment that fits the stand, but that trust has to rest on something the public can examine. Good objectives, transparent decisions, fair public processes, monitoring, and a willingness to return after the harvest all contribute to it. So does professional honesty about high grading, failed regeneration, uncertainty, and the places where leaving a stand alone may be the better choice.

The wider communication question, including who communicates forestry and how the profession represents itself in museums or other public settings, still deserves its own post. Within this narrower question, I keep coming back to visibility through time: show people the opening, explain what is supposed to regenerate, point out what was retained, and then come back when the forest has had enough time to respond.

Fifteen years after that Vermont harvest, we were pushing through pin cherry with half of the interview getting swallowed by muffled leaves, legacy trees still standing above us, and a new cohort working its way up underneath. The forester could describe what they had tried to do, and the forest gave us something against which to judge that explanation. Being able to show that forest to the average person, the layperson, or the non-forester is how social capital starts to become the social license a forester needs the next time a stand calls for a difficult cut.
