The northern hardwood forest is not a monolith, and once you start listing the pressures that affect it, the list gets long very quickly. There are invasive species, deer browse, existing stand conditions, climate, how hard a winter is, how heavy a harvest is, and it kind of goes on and on and on. All of these factors contribute to forest health. The people managing the forest add another level of variation through their goals, legal responsibilities, financial pressures, technical capacity, and ideas about what the forest should provide.

I eventually want to model how those ecological and human factors might shape the future of northern hardwood forests across the United States and Canada. Treating every landowner, forester, company, agency, and organization as its own actor would leave me with millions and millions and millions of data points to simulate. Stopping at five general ownership or management groups would make the model manageable, but it would also flatten differences that affect how forestry is practiced.

So I need something larger than one individual actor and more specific than a broad ownership group. That is what I am calling a practice bundle.

## The five groups get us partway there

Early in the project, we identified five general groups where different rule sets and jurisdictions take hold. Those are tribal entities; non-governmental organizations; private landowners, including both industrial timberland owners and non-industrial family forest landowners; public lands held by state, county, or local governments; and national public lands.

These groups give us a framework for thinking about who makes rules, who carries fiduciary responsibilities, and who has the authority to decide what happens on a particular piece of forest. A national public-land manager may work from formal forest plans, silvicultural plans, public participation requirements, and relatively concrete goals. State and county foresters operate under another set of policies, funding limitations, and public expectations. Tribal managers have their own governance structures and degrees of sovereignty. Non-governmental organizations include conservation groups, universities, and other institutions that may manage forests for research, education, habitat, recreation, timber, or some combination of those purposes.

Private ownership immediately branches in two very different directions. A large industrial timberland owner managing thousands of acres through a corporate decision-making structure is in a different position from a family forest landowner who might be most concerned about wildlife, recreation, taxes, timber income, privacy, or simply keeping the property in the family. Locally held public land also contains meaningful divisions. The financial concerns and fiduciary responsibilities of a county may differ from those of a state agency, even if both are categorized as public land.

Once those differences start appearing, you eventually come to the question I am trying to answer: how do people actually practice forestry among these groups? Are they beholden to a certain rule set, a recognizable set of values, or some combination of the two?

The amount of variability among family forest landowners is going to be huge, although I do not think the landowners themselves are simply chaotic. Their legal framework, access to incentives, cultural context, technical support, and willingness to work with a forester can change considerably from one state, province, or county to another. If I split every one of those differences into a separate category, I eventually end up back at the individual actor problem. Practice bundles are my approach to lump somewhat instead of having to split infinitely.

## What goes into a practice bundle

A practice bundle groups actors who operate under similar circumstances and appear likely to produce a similar range of management outcomes. I am looking for repeated patterns in what managers say they value, the rules and financial pressures they answer to, the technical support available to them, and the practices they tend to put on the ground.

The bundle is loosely representative. People and organizations within it will still vary, sometimes by a lot, and the bundle has to carry that variance into the model. Otherwise, I would be claiming a level of precision that our interviews cannot support.

I am still figuring out exactly where practice bundles sit in relation to established social science theories, and I do not want to force them under one theory simply to give the idea a theoretical label. There are, however, useful precedents for what I am trying to do. Researchers working with social-ecological models have developed ways to parameterize human agents using interviews and other empirical evidence. Agricultural researchers have also constructed relatively simple farmer typologies that connect land tenure, operation size, income, and information networks with environmental outcomes and policy scenarios ([Smajgl et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envsoft.2011.02.011); [Daloglu et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.agsy.2014.05.007)).

The theory of planned behavior offers another possible connection by describing how attitudes, social expectations, and perceived control contribute to intentions and behavior ([Ajzen 1991](https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-5978(91)90020-T)). I expect practice bundles to borrow from several of these traditions while remaining tied to the interviews, plans, regulations, and management records available for this project.

The practical unit I need is a group whose circumstances and approaches are similar enough that I can estimate the range of forest outcomes it might produce. Practice bundle is the term I am using for that unit.

## Governance and context decide where I lump

Governance structure is extremely important when deciding whether two actors belong in the same bundle. If two companies have similar decision-making frameworks, face similar regulations, and describe the same financial pressures and management goals, it makes sense to consider merging them in the model.

Suppose industrial representatives in northern Michigan and Wisconsin repeatedly describe finances and keeping a mill operational as the bottom line. If they also operate within similar regional markets and regulatory settings, that begins to look like a like set of values backed by similar governance. The companies may be operating across a political boundary, but their management approaches could still belong in the same practice bundle.

Larger private landowners in New Hampshire and Vermont may present a similar case. The states share parts of a climatic, ecological, and regional context, although their regulations and incentive programs would still need to be compared before I treated them as one group. Family forest landowners may need to remain separated by state even when a bundle for larger industrial owners can cross the same boundary. Tax programs, legal obligations, access to technical assistance, and the culture surrounding private land can all influence what a small landowner is willing or able to do.

Ontario presents another version of the same problem. Family forest landowners in the southern Carolinian forest region, where forests are embedded within a largely agricultural and more densely settled area, may behave differently from landowners in the northern core hardwood region. New Brunswick, Quebec, and Ontario may each require separate treatment. Ontario itself may need more than one family forest bundle. The context has to remain compartmentalized where it affects management.

The interviews are how I will begin deciding whether these similarities are supported well enough to use in the model. The interview manual covers a lot of bases, so repeated pressures, values, and descriptions of practice become visible across conversations. Hearing several industrial representatives describe the same financial bottom line tells me something about their motivations. Similar governance on the back end helps explain how those motivations are manifested through management.

Written forest plans, silvicultural plans, regulations, and management records can then help determine whether the stated approaches match formal goals and operations. When those different forms of evidence line up, merging actors becomes more defensible. Contradictions may call for a separate bundle or a wider range of behavior within the existing one.

## The point is to explain outcomes

A practice bundle is supposed to help capture the outcomes that can be produced through management. I have much less confidence that it can predict the exact decision an individual landowner, company, or forester will make. There will always be an amount of variance within each bundle, so the model needs to work with ranges and distributions rather than assign one fixed action to everybody in the group.

Once the bundles are assembled, I can estimate what percentage of the northern hardwood land base each one represents. Those bundles can then act as the agents in what is basically an agent-based model built from lumped groups. From there, we can pull levers and see how the expected outcomes change.

One scenario might reduce deer browsing pressure within Vermont by 20 percent through additional deer harvesting and trace what happens to regeneration. Another might provide incentives for invasive-species control across the northern hardwood range. Other scenarios could increase funding, technical support, physical labor support, or participation in incentive programs for one group of managers.

The practice bundle does not tell us the exact decision that every person makes after receiving an incentive. It lets us estimate how the distribution of management practices might shift within the group and what that shift could mean for the forest. That response to incentives is a major part of what the bundles need to capture.

At the tree biology level, I am particularly concerned with regeneration and species diversity. Foresters often picture successful regeneration as a sign of a forest with vitality and some capacity to persist through change. Deer browsing affects regeneration differently across species and seedling sizes, and recent research from forests in the northeastern United States found particularly strong negative relationships for sugar maple ([Harris et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foreco.2025.122906)).

Species diversity may also help forests resist damage from pests and pathogens, although the outcome depends on which species occur together, the identity of neighboring trees, and environmental conditions such as drought ([Guyot et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsbl.2015.1037); [Field et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/1365-2745.13397)). A diversity value by itself will not tell the whole story, but diversity is still one of the forest characteristics I want the model to track.

Timber production, clean water, wildlife habitat, and broader ecosystem health also belong in the outcome set. Some of these outcomes have more salience with the general public, while others speak more directly to economic interests or forestry practice. When we come down to the tree biology of it all, regeneration and species diversity are probably two of the clearest indications that the future forest is still being assembled.

## The error has to remain visible

Bundling actors will flatten some meaningful differences and misrepresent some of the variance within a group. I have to accept a certain amount of that error to make the model feasible, but I cannot hide it behind false precision. Evidence will be uneven. Interviews, written plans, regulations, and management records will support some assumptions well, while other parameters will remain estimates.

Our interview dataset has a specific blind spot. We are generally not interviewing foresters or operators who high-grade forests, in part because people associated with poorly documented or widely criticized practices usually have little reason to volunteer for a research interview. We have to assume those actors and practices are present, but it is difficult to determine their prevalence. They exist in a space beyond sufficient documentation.

The model therefore needs some way to acknowledge management that is underrepresented in the interviews. Treating the interview sample as the complete actor population would produce a cleaner model, but it would also misrepresent what is happening on the ground.

Some bundles will be much easier to construct. A U.S. Forest Service bundle may have written planning requirements, fairly concrete goals, technical staff, and records that keep the assumptions relatively narrow. Many of the goals and practices are written down in numbers, which reduces the amount of estimation required.

The family forest landowner bundle is the chaotic practice one, at least from a modeling perspective. Landowner priorities, acreage, technical knowledge, participation in incentive programs, consultation with foresters, and willingness to conduct active management may vary widely. That bundle will probably require broader ranges and some form of randomization to capture different priorities and levels of forestry involvement.

I also need to leave room for management that does not work. More incentive money or additional silvicultural activity will not automatically improve forest health. A practice bundle should be able to produce limited improvement, no improvement, or an unintended outcome. Otherwise, the model would simply restate our preferences instead of helping us test them.

## Starting with something explainable

No model is ever going to be perfect, and no survey ever has a 100 percent response rate. In the absence of a complete dataset covering every person and organization that manages northern hardwoods, practice bundles are how I am trying to make the model feasible while keeping its assumptions visible.

We have to start somewhere, and it helps if the starting units are explainable enough for people to argue with. Someone looking at a practice bundle should be able to ask why those actors were grouped, whether the boundary is in the right place, whether the assumed variance is wide enough, and what evidence might require the bundle to change. That gives us a model we can discuss without pretending that we have interviewed or observed every agent on the forest.

For now, this first post is meant to define the idea. Part Two can describe how practice bundles fit together across jurisdictions and the northern hardwood range. A later post can walk through the construction of an individual bundle, beginning with a comparatively concrete case such as a U.S. Forest Service manager and eventually moving toward the much more variable family forest landowner bundle. That last one will take a lot more brain power.
