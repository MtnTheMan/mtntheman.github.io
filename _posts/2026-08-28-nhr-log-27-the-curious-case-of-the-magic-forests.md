Another concept that I really want to talk about is what I'm going to call the curious case of the magic forests. As I interview foresters across the range of the northern hardwoods, I sometimes come across foresters who exist in a space where everything seems to be going alright. Browse pressure from deer or moose is relatively low. Invasive species, forest pests, and pathogens are present at manageable levels or have not become the defining problem in the stand. The soils are pretty good, the biodiversity looks pretty good, and managers are still able to retain the composition of their forests.

These kinds of forests can start to seem almost mythical. Many foresters will call them magic forests, but I think it could be helpful to say more clearly what a magic forest actually looks like. The word *magic* is descriptive in its own way, although it can also hide the ecological and management conditions that allow the forest to keep responding as well as it does.

## What I mean by a magic forest

When I use the phrase *magic forest*, I am usually describing a forest with a lot of diversity in species and a lot of structural diversity, both horizontally and vertically. Horizontal structure describes how trees, gaps, regeneration, and other forest conditions vary across the stand. Vertical structure describes the different levels within the forest, including the way the canopy is tiered rather than concentrated at one height.

A magic forest will also have healthy and diverse regeneration of different species, especially what we would call advanced regeneration. Those young trees are already established beneath the canopy and are in a position to respond when a harvest or other disturbance creates more light and growing space.

It also helps if the site has been worked for a while and continues to be responsive to different harvest actions. Some magic forests are continuously worked woods with long management histories. They have gone through repeated entries and disturbances, but they still have the ability to regenerate. The species may shift, and an individual stand may respond differently from the one next to it, but the forest continues to produce enough regeneration to sustain a diverse hardwood stand.

There are many factors that can quickly prohibit a magic forest from being magic. If any one part of the equation becomes badly imbalanced, managers can have a hard time regenerating successfully, or at least sufficiently. Heavy deer or moose browse can remove desirable seedlings before they recruit into larger size classes. Invasive plants can occupy the growing space that becomes available after a harvest. Pests and pathogens can alter composition by removing a species that previously played a large role in the stand.

An invasive species entering a rich site can be especially hard to manage. Foresters may be caught flat-footed by a new introduction, or they may lack the resources and planning needed to respond before it becomes established. A forest can appear fine at the broader scale while an individual unit is already losing the characteristics that would have made it magic.

## Inherited momentum

I keep wondering how much of a magic forest comes from luck and how much comes from active management and thoughtful, applied management considerations. I have a hard time separating the two.

Most foresters are humble in recognizing that really good conditions were often already there. They inherited a forest that had retained a lot of momentum, along with conditions that could continue producing a good response after disturbance. Maybe the site was productive. Maybe browse pressure had remained low enough for regeneration to persist. Maybe the stand had kept a useful mixture of species through previous entries. Whatever the particular combination was, the manager started with more options than someone working in a degraded stand.

Managers can create the right conditions or drive a stand toward greater resilience, but many of the foresters I talk with are constantly fighting the longer fight of improving resiliency from a baseline that started out poor. Many managers report that they are trying to improve conditions after some earlier period of degradation. That starting point changes the work. Preserving a functioning regeneration layer is different from trying to rebuild one after it has been missing for decades.

For foresters who do not face those same problems, the explanation may be that the forest was already resilient from the start. That does not make the current management irrelevant. Retaining diversity and ensuring that conditions for good regeneration persist over time still take careful work, and the appropriate work can look very different depending on who is managing the land and what the current threats call for.

There may also be pieces of institutional wisdom that have carried responsible harvest practices across generations. Managers learn how particular forests respond, retain the practices that have worked, and pass some of that knowledge to whoever comes next. If an area has a functioning timber economy, that probably helps support continuous management and creates more opportunities for foresters to remain involved over time.

A good timber economy does not guarantee responsible forestry, but places that have lacked foresters for long periods may have a harder time maintaining that continuity. If an area experiences decades upon decades of poor forestry practice, recovery can take a long time, and the characteristics of a magic forest can disappear pretty quickly.

I want to believe there is an amount of institutional inertia in doing good management and seeing the products of good management over time. Foresters inherit favorable conditions, continue working with those conditions in mind, and leave a stand that remains responsive for the next manager. Some of what we call magic may be the accumulated result of that positive momentum.

## Magic on this acre, but not the next

Even within an area that people might broadly describe as a magic forest, conditions can vary widely from stand to stand. One part of a county may have relatively little deer browse while another part has enough browse to alter regeneration. An invasive species may be concentrated in one unit and absent from a neighboring one. Harvest histories, ownership, soils, access, and available management resources can also change over short distances.

So, I think we have to allow for the possibility that the magic forest exists in this acre but not in an immediately adjacent acre.

One example I keep turning over comes from state-of-resource forest inventory data around northern Maine and the border with New Brunswick, in the general area between Presque Isle, Maine, and Woodstock, New Brunswick. In the data I reviewed, sapling stocking appeared better on the New Brunswick side than it did across the border in Maine. I do not have interview evidence that explains the pattern, and I would not want to assign a cause based on that observation alone.

The border leaves me wondering how much national or provincial policy, wildlife management, markets, ownership, harvesting history, and the movement of forest pests or pathogens might contribute to the difference. Each possible explanation comes with its own complication, and a political boundary does not create a clean ecological experiment. I would need much more evidence before using any of those factors to explain the apparent stocking pattern.

Nobody I spoke with in New Brunswick described their forest as a magic forest. In Vermont, though, people did use the phrase when talking about the regeneration they were seeing. I did not have an immediately adjacent, poorly performing forest in Vermont that would create a clean comparison, so these examples do not fit together neatly. They still show why I am hesitant to treat a magic forest as a stable regional category.

The word *magic* can obscure history, policy, markets, and the ecological factors that produce good conditions. It can dilute our understanding of what creates those conditions and how easily they might be lost. Policy and markets contribute to the health of a northern hardwood area, as does the ability to practice good silviculture with guidance from landowners, foresters, logging contractors, agencies, and other parties. Site conditions and the absence of a particular threat may also give some forests an advantage that managers elsewhere never had.

There is probably some luck mixed into all of this. I do not know how much, and I suspect the answer changes from place to place. But when I use the phrase *magic forest*, I am describing a forest with a lot of choices still available within it: different species, varied structure, advanced regeneration, and enough responsiveness to continue regenerating after disturbance.

For now, the phrase gives me a place to begin, while the conditions behind it are what I want to understand. I want to know what each forest inherited, how managers carried those conditions forward, and why they can disappear between one acre and the next. The forest may look magic when we first walk into it. Its history is probably doing more of the work than the word suggests.
