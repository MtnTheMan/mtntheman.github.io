Day 2 put two woods next to each other in my head. In the larger, contiguous timber block, sugar maple regeneration was not hypothetical. Seedlings were up, the understory had depth, and deer browse looked like one pressure among several rather than the whole story. The day before, in a small woodlot ringed by crop fields, the lower layer was almost empty. No sugar maple seedlings. No saplings. Same broader region, very different capacity to take a hit. That contrast is the point of this post: patch size and farm-country context change how deer pressure lands on northern hardwood regeneration.

The surrounding agricultural matrix can feed deer outside the forest and then concentrate their winter pressure inside it. In fragmented parts of Southern Ontario and the Upper Midwest, crop fields carry deer through the summer. When winter comes, those same deer need cover, and isolated woodlots become obvious shelter. Augustine reported winter deer densities of twenty-five to thirty-five individuals per square kilometer in small remnants, with heavy browsing on native spring wildflowers and woody seedlings (Augustine, 1997). The problem is that those deer numbers are not being carried by the woodlot alone. The surrounding fields help maintain the population, while the winter damage gets focused inside the small patch (Augustine, 1998). Larger forest blocks do not remove browse pressure, but they give deer more room to spread out, so damage is less likely to pile onto the same few acres.

Edges make that concentration sharper. White-tailed deer already use forest edges for travel and feeding, and a small farm woodlot is almost all edge. Even moderate deer numbers can stop young sugar maple and oak from recruiting into taller classes (Alverson et al., 1988). Waller et al. tied chronic deer browse to nearly half of the observed vegetation change in Upper Midwest forests over several decades (Waller et al., 2014). Over time, the woods lose the layer that is supposed to become the next canopy. Preferred seedlings get clipped back, less palatable plants like ferns and grasses gain space, and the stand starts to look open and park-like under aging overstory trees (Horsley et al., 2003; Waller et al., 2014).

This is visible in local reports, not just in the field notes from this trip. The Sifton Bog woodland assessment in London, Ontario, described an upland forest stripped from about fifteen centimeters up to the deer browse line, with browse-resistant invasive species such as buckthorn left behind (Thames River Conservation Authority, 2011). That is close to what I saw in the small field-surrounded woodlots: after canopy disturbance, almost no hardwood regeneration made it into the next size class.

The formal literature still seems thin on direct, landscape-scale comparisons of patch size, connectivity, and browse severity. But the available pattern is hard to ignore. Small forest patches in farm country need active deer management, fencing, or some other protection if they are going to keep regenerating. Without that, the overstory can look like a forest while the next forest is already missing.
