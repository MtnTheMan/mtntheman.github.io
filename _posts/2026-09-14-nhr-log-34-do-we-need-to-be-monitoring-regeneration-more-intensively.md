If a forester is formally monitoring regeneration, the work involves stopping in a stand and counting what is happening close to the ground. Seedlings and saplings can be separated into different height classes and species, then recorded across enough plots to estimate the number of stems per acre. Those numbers can be compared with the stocking levels recommended in management guidebooks, providing some indication of whether the stand has enough regeneration to recruit a future cohort. It is a replicable process that can be compared among stands, but it is also a time-costly and man-hour-costly endeavor.

While I was on the road talking with foresters, I heard just about the full range of approaches to regeneration monitoring. Some managers had a formal process. Others had a text box in an inventory system where they could indicate whether the regeneration looked good or bad, almost like a green-light or yellow-light indicator rather than a formal counting measure. Many managers said that they had no regeneration monitoring, or at least that they were not required to monitor it whatsoever.

The more common approach was what I would call visual regeneration monitoring. I hate to use the term, and I think it needs to appear in heavy quotation marks, but this could also be described as “windshield silviculture” or “windshield inventorying.” A forester might glance at a stand from the road or think about regeneration while walking through the stand and inventorying it for another purpose. They were not arduously measuring whether advance regeneration was present across the stand, but it was still their intention to take it into account and consider whether it would sufficiently recruit into the overstory.

That type of visual assessment can contain a great deal of professional judgment, especially for someone who knows the land and can recognize a regeneration problem quickly. The limitation is that it does not give us a scientifically rigorous or replicable measure, and it becomes difficult to compare the condition of regeneration among stands that may be under the same manager or across different ownerships. A green-light or yellow-light judgment may end up in a database, while an informal observation may remain entirely in the forester’s memory. Both foresters may have looked at regeneration, but they have produced very different kinds of information.

Formal regeneration monitoring, when it did occur, was most often discussed as something conducted after a harvest, sometimes in connection with Sustainable Forestry Initiative certification. That timing can help document how a stand responded to an entry, although it does less to inform the prescription that produced the response in the first place.

Advance regeneration describes the forest’s next cohort already being in place before a harvest gets underway. I want to be precise about the wording here: it is advance regeneration, rather than advanced regeneration, because the seedlings and saplings have arrived in advance of the harvest. When that cohort is present, a forester can conduct operations around it, try to avoid damaging it during removals or entries, and give those trees an opportunity to grow into the light spaces left behind.

Much of the older silvicultural guidance for northern hardwoods assumes that hundreds or thousands of seedlings and saplings will remain after a harvest and eventually take the place of the overstory. Management guidebooks can recommend far, far, far more stocking of seedlings and regeneration than what we may actually have in the forest. The guidance generally assumes that regeneration will occur as intended, without fully accounting for the pressures affecting the regenerating layer in many northern hardwood stands. As we work through the Northern Hardwoods Resilience project, we are finding a gap between that expectation and what managers are working with on the ground.

If advance regeneration is absent before an entry, removing the overstory is unlikely to make the desired cohort magically appear. There may be too little seed source, or seedlings may be getting repeatedly browsed by deer or moose. Invasive plants can occupy the growing space that becomes available, and less valuable or non-merchantable species can overshade species that have traditionally formed a merchantable northern hardwood canopy. The result may be a stand that moves toward a composition or condition that neither the forester nor the landowner intended, and in some cases there may eventually be a different land use altogether.

I feel like the absence of advance regeneration should normally tell the forester that more seed source needs to be left behind to establish a future cohort. Ideally, the prescription would also leave behind better phenotypes that can contribute to that cohort. But the landowner may still expect a harvest, and the forester has to balance that present expectation against what will remain in the stand afterward. Planting northern hardwoods is considered one of the most costly ways to reestablish them, while most regeneration in these forests has traditionally occurred through natural establishment, so it would be extremely difficult to assume that planting later could easily repair the absence of regeneration going into the entry. It becomes a bad balancing act and can turn into a lose-lose situation for the landowner and forester in the long run.

Even with all of that, I do not know that requiring intensive regeneration monitoring across every acre is the solution. Foresters are already burdened with a great deal to do and too little time to do it. In [NHR Log 4: Span of Control in Forestry](https://www.mtntheman.com/northern-hardwoods/research/travel/2026/05/24/span-of-control-in-forestry.html) and [NHR Log 4.5: Updated Span of Control Calculator](https://www.mtntheman.com/northern-hardwoods/research/travel/2026/05/31/span-of-control-calculator-v2.html), I worked through how much time it takes to manage a given number of acres successfully, and adding intensive regeneration monitoring throughout that process could easily skyrocket those estimates. A forester could end up spending more time counting what is happening on the ground than actively managing, preparing contracts and bids, or communicating with peers about what successful regeneration could be spurred by.

I think those conversations can be more useful for producing successful regeneration than requiring individual foresters to conduct counts all the way across the board. Research initiatives already collect information about regeneration, and forest inventory and analysis programs can help fill gaps that an individual forester cannot reasonably cover. The academic and forestry research communities can also help us predict what regeneration is likely to look like under different stand conditions, browsing pressures, and harvesting approaches, while identifying the situations where the uncertainty is high enough to require a closer field assessment.

Research programs and state guidance principles also need to account for the lack of advance regeneration rather than continuing from an assumption that the next cohort will be there. Academics can do a better job of setting new baselines for how we approach northern hardwood silviculture, allowing us to maintain resilient stands while still producing a reasonable yield. Updated guidance could encourage practices that provide something of an insurance policy when a harvest proceeds without an adequate future cohort already in place, with the retention of additional seed source being one possible part of that approach.

I do not think we should have a normative, established rule requiring intensive monitoring everywhere, since there may be many forests that do not need that level of monitoring. My answer to the title question is still “maybe not,” at least if more intensive monitoring means establishing plots and counting stems on every acre. We need approaches that help both the forester and the forests, while recognizing that there is a gap in our knowledge and expectations for how northern hardwood stands respond after an entry.

There will be places where formal plots are needed and others where an experienced visual assessment may provide enough information, especially if the assessment is recorded and connected to the prescription. Research and regional inventory programs can help identify which forests need a closer look, rather than leaving each forester to reconstruct the entire regeneration picture independently. I keep coming back to the idea that foresters need enough information to avoid preparing a prescription as though the next cohort is already there when, on the ground, it simply is not.
