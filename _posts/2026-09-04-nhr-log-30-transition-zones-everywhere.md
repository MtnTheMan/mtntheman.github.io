Is anywhere in the northern hardwoods not in some form of a transition zone?

No matter where I was on this long road trip, it felt like there was always another forest type being managed for, with very few exceptions. The northern hardwoods were there, but they sat inside overlapping Venn diagram layers with other forests that might intrude, become the dominant system, or become the thing a forester spent most of their time thinking about. I started with this question as a way to map ecological overlaps. After hearing managers talk about their work across the range, I also started to see transitions in markets, experience, and management attention.

At the northernmost Canadian extent, these interactions included the taiga, or boreal forest, with the Acadian hardwood system entering the picture farther east. Across the central parts and latitudes of the region, there were red pine systems and plantations. Michigan had aspen and poplar pulp systems, while the western part of the range in Minnesota ran into drier prairie transition areas and plains hardwoods that were not quite the same as the northern hardwoods we were seeing elsewhere. In New Jersey, the overlapping systems included pitch pine plantations and barrens.

Other competing forest types were other "hardwood" systems that are normally treated differently because of their light regimes and sensitivity to disturbance. Oak-hickory systems become prominent through southern New England, along coastal areas, and near the southern and eastern reaches of the northern hardwood range. The Allegheny hardwoods shape management in Pennsylvania and southern New York, while the Carolinian forests enter the southernmost parts of Ontario. Pretty much everywhere we went, there were mixtures or overlaps with another forest type, whether that overlap had a widely used name or not, and foresters could not help but talk about some of their experience managing those other forests.

Some of what I am calling overlaps are extensive forest categories in their own right and could be considered as large as the extant northern hardwood forest. I do not think that makes northern hardwoods too broad or any less useful as a category than FIA's maple/beech/birch group or other broad stand descriptions. Northern hardwoods still gives us a way to describe a major forest resource across a large region, but interpreting the state of management requires us to consider what might be weighing on managers in different parts of that region and what the local mixture says about the expertise and applied experience they have developed.

The competition became most noticeable when markets drove foresters toward commodities other than hardwoods. Hardwood markets really are not what they used to be across the range. There was a heyday, as recently as the 1990s in some places, when moving hardwoods was seen as a guaranteed profitable venture, and when producing quality logs and regeneration was simultaneously treated as a comparatively easy thing across much of the northern hardwood belt.

As hardwood profit margins shrank, the science also indicated that getting a good harvest and successful regeneration required more judgment about the individual stand and site. Other forest types and plantations continually became more attractive as work sites. Pine systems and aspen/poplar pulp systems can be more commercially viable, and many are managed through plantation or clearcutting regimes that may require less heavy lifting from the manager or planner. With the market weaker and the path toward a good northern hardwood outcome becoming harder, those other systems could move ahead in the working portfolio.

Every one of those systems still takes institutional wisdom and time to master. A manager has to learn the specific problems and elements of management for red pine, aspen, oak-hickory, pitch pine, or whichever systems occur within the area where they work. Their portfolio is going to reflect what the markets support, what their employer or clients ask them to do, and where they have been able to build experience. I cannot blame a manager for building a portfolio around that, even when it means another system can win the day of their attention.

At some stops, I saw hesitation that could lead to delayed northern hardwood work, and I heard managers describe default approaches that might be carried from one job to another despite differences in stand or site conditions. Elsewhere, managers seemed resigned to doing the most essential or responsive work in northern hardwoods, particularly work involving invasive species, pests, or pathogens. Some simply reflected on the fact that time was finite and getting to their northern hardwoods was not always the focus, although they knew they had some.

I do not want to imply that copy-paste prescriptions are normal basically anywhere. Most foresters are very cognizant of the differences from stand to stand, and many take a pseudo-experimental approach to the northern hardwood jobs they do. They adjust the work to their experience and education, the conditions in the stand, what they have learned from earlier jobs, and the expectations of their employers or clients. The prescription may build from something familiar, but the manager is still making decisions according to the place and circumstances in front of them.

When I look at the state of management across the region, I want to account for that background before interpreting a treatment, a delay, or an apparent gap in expertise. A prescription that looks simple from the outside may sit within a much larger portfolio of responsibilities and experience. I still think critical review has a place, especially where current approaches are failing to produce the regeneration or stand conditions that managers intended. I also do not want to overly criticize best efforts to manage northern hardwoods under constraints that researchers may have an easier time describing than managers have solving during their daily work.

People do not get into forestry without caring about the woods or forests as a resource. They certainly do not get into it with a money grab as the sole purpose. Markets shape where the work goes, and a manager can care about northern hardwoods while still spending more of their working life in another forest system that has stronger markets or occupies more of their employer's attention.

I think there is a lot of incremental progress possible when we take silvicultural practice and improve it by applying learned principles and sharing new and improved practices as we see them work across the whole region. When a practice is shared, it also needs to carry enough information about the stand, the manager's objectives, and the conditions under which it worked for someone else to decide whether it fits their own situation. The regional work, as I see it, is to understand those differences well enough that a good idea learned in one place can be carried somewhere else without pretending that the two places are the same.
