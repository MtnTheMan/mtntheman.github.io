One of the most common comments I get from foresters as I travel across the Northern Hardwood range is that their markets are poor. The specific reason changes depending on where I am, but the general complaint is almost one of the universal truths of these interviews. With very few exceptions, foresters express some concern about their market, its sustainability, or its long-term viability.

Usually, the concern is not that there are no wood products being sold. It is that the available markets do not line up very well with the wood that good forestry produces. Some regional markets are built around softwoods or pine products. Some pulp mills use mixes that do not create much demand for hardwood pulp. In other places, the missing outlet might be veneer, pallet wood, or another specific quality of material. The result is that a forester can sell part of what comes out of a stand but is left with a conundrum over the remaining wood fiber.

That conundrum has a direct effect on what can happen in the woods. If the market does not support pulp, low-value material, or some other part of a harvest, the treatment can become economically and operationally infeasible even when it makes sense silviculturally. Foresters are then left trying to manage a stand around the products they can sell, which may contribute to continued degradation or make it harder to maintain a good Northern Hardwood resource over time.

As I was driving across the whole of the Northern Hardwood range, I was also getting to see a lot of different contexts for these markets. A product that moves relatively easily in one part of the range may have nowhere to go somewhere else. Even in places where foresters described the markets as generally good, they usually identified some weak category or limitation.

That repeated observation is where the idea for this map came from. This post is basically a methods defense for something I have been working on in the background: how could we map the strengths and weaknesses of Northern Hardwood markets across the entire range, and what would we have to include before claiming that the map represents market health? I am not ready to produce the map quite yet. I want to make sure the product is well enough developed to push out, but I think the premise is ready to lay out.

## Defining a healthy Northern Hardwood market

My first definition of a healthy market was that a forester can sell every product they have in their forests. I would qualify that a little after thinking through it. A healthy market means that a forester has some usable outlet for each type and quality of material produced by the forests in that area, with enough flexibility to manage different stands without constantly running into quotas, purchasing limits, or other constraints that make a treatment impractical.

The product categories would have to cover the most common material coming out of Northern Hardwood stands. Veneer would be one of them and is generally the most valuable hardwood product in the mix, followed by different grades of sawlogs, pallet wood, pulp, biomass, plywood, and smaller or more specialized markets. The categories would probably need to account for species, grade, size, and intended use as well. A mill that accepts one species or grade cannot necessarily provide an outlet for everything else that comes off the same job.

At the same time, market health has to be measured against the forest resource that is actually present. Near the edges of the Northern Hardwood range, we do not always see the quality of hardwood that would produce the highest classes of sawlogs or much veneer. A missing veneer market means something different in a place with abundant veneer-quality material than it does in a place that produces very little of it. Markets have to be built around the resources located wherever those Northern Hardwood stands are.

That means the resource itself would be one major part of the model. We could use tools such as the U.S. Forest Service's tree-mapping resources to estimate where different species, volumes, qualities, or potential product classes occur across the range. State inventories and other regional reporting could provide another view of what is growing and what is already moving through the market. I think that information should be possible to gather, although parts of it may be opaque and we would need to make some assumptions when translating forest conditions into possible wood products.

Those assumptions would need to remain visible. The map should distinguish an estimate of potential veneer-quality material from a measured volume of veneer logs moving through a market, for instance, because those are different things. The first describes what the resource may be capable of producing. The second describes what people are currently harvesting and selling, which may already reflect the weakness of the market we are trying to measure.

## A mill on a map is not necessarily an outlet

Once we have some idea of what the forest can produce, the next problem is figuring out which mills and buyers are available in practice. Simply placing a point for every mill on a map would not answer that question. A useful outlet has to be within an economically reasonable hauling distance, accept the relevant species and grades, have demand for that material, and be able to receive it in enough volume to influence management.

Transportation distance would change by product. Veneer can travel much farther because its value relative to its weight can support a longer haul. Low-value pulp, biomass, and similar materials usually cannot carry the same transportation cost. Rather than drawing one fixed radius around every mill, I picture a road-based model in which the possible sourcing area changes depending on the product and perhaps on other assumptions about cost.

The mill information would also have to include accepted species, grade requirements, common product mixes, mill capacity, and the typical volume moving through the facility. Demand matters at the level of individual species and quantities. A mill may buy hardwood while having little interest in the particular mix that a nearby stand produces.

Operating status adds another complication. Some mills are open but operating at reduced capacity. Others use quotas or temporarily limit purchases. A mill might change what it accepts while continuing to appear the same in an older database, so the model would need to recognize that the existence of a facility and the existence of an active market are separate measurements.

There are also many small mills scattered throughout the Northern Hardwood range that do specialized work. Their activity may be recorded in the Forest Service Timber Products Output database, state databases, or tax records that capture the economic activity of the mill itself. They may not handle the volume of a large facility, but in some places they could be the difference between having an outlet for a particular product and having nowhere for it to go.

Putting those pieces together would give us a way to compare the amount and type of material a region can produce with the amount and type that nearby markets can receive. The gap between those two sides is probably where market health begins to become measurable.

## Mapping utilization and underutilization

I think the map should include both a composite score and separate layers for individual products. The composite score could describe how much of the available product mix is being delivered to a market, or how much appears to be underutilized across the Northern Hardwood range. A place where most available products have realistic outlets would show stronger market health, while a place with large volumes of stranded or underused material would show weaker health.

That could become a heat map of health, with warmer areas representing generally good markets for several products and cooler areas representing poor or incomplete markets. But I would not want the composite score to become the whole product. If it did, the score could hide the specific problem a forester is trying to describe.

The individual layers would show whether the weakness involves veneer, sawlogs, pallet material, pulp, biomass, or another product. A region could look reasonably healthy overall while having no practical market for low-value hardwood pulp. Another area might move large quantities of pulp but have poor outlets for high-quality sawlogs. Both places have a market problem, but they do not have the same problem and should not receive the same explanation.

I would also like the map to be interactive enough that users can mess with the values and see what changes. A forester could adjust the acceptable hauling distance for a product, account for a mill operating under a quota, or examine how the regional score changes when a specialized facility is included or removed. That would make the assumptions easier to see and would help explain why it is so hard for someone in a given location to sell a particular product.

The same map could expose areas where there is a gap in the existing market and a lot of potential feedstock available for a mill. That would still be an early indication rather than proof that a new facility would work. Mill development depends on costs, labor, infrastructure, financing, and other conditions that this type of map would not fully represent. Still, the overlap between underused material and missing processing capacity would be useful to see.

What I keep coming back to is how poor markets seemed to exist almost everywhere I traveled in Northern Hardwoods, with very few exceptions. In places where markets were reported as good, there were still weaknesses in some product categories. That makes me wonder whether "poor markets" is too broad a description for what foresters are experiencing. The market may be poor for pulp, veneer, a particular species, a particular grade, or a particular volume, while functioning reasonably well for everything else.

A range-wide map of utilization could give us a way to separate those problems. It could connect what is growing in the forest with what mills are buying, how far the material can reasonably travel, and where capacity or purchasing limitations interrupt that movement. It would also give foresters something they could use to describe the challenges they already encounter when trying to move wood.

For now, I want to get the concept and its assumptions out where people can think about them. The finished model should tell us more than whether a region has mills or sells forest products. It should show which parts of the Northern Hardwood resource are being used, which parts are being left behind, and what specific weakness creates the conundrum for the forester trying to do good work in the woods.
