I am about 36 hours from doing another interview when I get an email saying that the forester I planned to meet can no longer talk. Cancellations happen on a road trip like this. Weather has impacts, and the foresters I am interviewing along this long trip all have lives as well. All of a sudden, I have an afternoon that has opened up. This has become the rainy-day alternative plan for the Northern Hardwoods Resilience megatrip. Oftentimes I go spend time in the woods, since that is what this trip is all about. But if there is a place nearby that can help me understand what forestry and logging look like in the region where I am traveling, I try to make that stop too.

In the Adirondacks, I went to the Adirondack Experience. In New Brunswick, I visited the Central New Brunswick Woodmen's Museum, which was way larger than I expected. In Algonquin Park, I found the Algonquin Logging Museum. The museum itself did not normally open until later in June when I was there, but its interpretive trail was still available, with a booklet that followed a series of numbered posts through the history of logging in the park.

After about half a dozen local history museums, forestry museums, logging museums, and interpretive sites, I have started to think of these places as locations where forestry looks in the mirror. Each one has made decisions about the tools, people, photographs, machines, stories, and ideas that represent forestry in that region. I like going through them with one question in mind: How does forestry describe itself here? More often than not, it describes itself in the past tense.

There are good reasons for that. The historical material is visually compelling. You have black-and-white photographs of massive log jams, horse-skidding teams, river drives, dangerous camps, early chain skidders, and the first cable skidder models. You have hard-nosed, rough, individualistic pioneers doing work that clearly drove the engine of early America and Canada. Staring at an ArcGIS terminal does not give a museum quite the same raw material as a crew trying to clear a log jam in a river.

The trouble comes when the story more or less stops there. Some museums offer a nod toward present-day forestry, but even the present-day portions of the exhibits I have seen were often developed in the late 1990s or early 2000s. There are certainly people still using equipment from that period, so the equipment belongs in the story. It becomes a problem when it stands in for the whole current profession, especially when the exhibit has no cut-to-length machines, very little about current planning tools, and almost no sense of how safety, efficiency, or different levels of automation have changed the work.

The forestry exhibit at the Adirondack Experience gave me the most literal sign I could have asked for. Forestry and active timber harvesting are represented in several places across the museum, but this particular exhibit is specifically supposed to explain forestry, and the museum is planning to replace it because of its dated nature. It briefly shows equipment from around the turn of the 21st century and includes a list of universities that teach forestry, although that list appeared to be many decades out of date. I believe Michigan State University was still shown under one of its earlier names. Walking through it, I could see why the exhibit had reached the end of its useful life. Elsewhere in the Adirondack Experience, there was a newer and much more interactive treatment of logging history that I thought worked really well. You could experience something of what forestry had been like rather than reading a series of labels. Even there, though, the experience passed up an opportunity to carry the story into the present. It gave visitors a stronger feel for forestry's history without giving them much sense of what forestry looks and feels like now.

I keep wondering what the public takes away from that stopping point. Does a visitor leave thinking that forestry was something of yesteryear, a rough activity from an earlier and more reductive civilization? I do not know if reductive is the right word. Caveman may be closer to the feeling. Forestry becomes something we needed when we were building towns, clearing land, and driving logs down rivers, followed by an assumption that it should have less of a role in a modern society. Modern societies still need wood products. People in Europe need diapers just like Americans need diapers, and many countries that we would readily describe as modern have developed economies with forest products as an active part of them. I think about Finland as a glowing example, although Finland also has a simpler forest system to operate within than the northern hardwood belt. Northern hardwoods are complicated. We are working with more species, more varied stand conditions, uneven markets, and a long list of ecological and operational considerations that do not reduce easily into one repeatable system.

The United States also grows far more wood than it harvests, and our forests remain an intrinsic part of the American experience. Forests are woven into the cultural identity of many regions of the United States, and the maple leaf makes the Canadian connection difficult to miss. Yet when forestry is displayed as an "oh, that is kind of nice" activity from yesteryear, we lose an opportunity to explain how working forests still fit into those identities and economies. All in all, forestry may not be as well captured or as well represented as other industries that have also modernized. We have a shortage of communication about what forestry in the 21st century really looks and feels like.

We are entirely dependent on how we represent forestry and logging. The way we describe forestry, and the level of esteem in which we hold it, heavily influences the public support available for proactive forest management. A strong forest industry usually has some inertia that allows it to remain strong. Where the industry has faded, the museum may become one of the few places where the public encounters it, and the story on display often concentrates on what the industry used to be. Of course, museums reach only the people who choose to enter them. Most people who interact with the woods will do so in the woods, where community-based interpretive organizations can also help them understand what they are seeing, but a museum is still a deliberate public statement about what forestry has been and what it is now.

Forestry in the 21st century is a profession, with the emphasis on professional. It is science-based. It uses GIS, modern harvesting systems, carefully developed prescriptions, professional standards, and machinery that has benefited from international development and collaboration, including work by Scandinavian and German companies. It is meant to create sustainable harvests of the things people need to live, while maintaining forests over time. That work can happen only where there are enabling conditions: trained people, viable markets, suitable equipment, public understanding, and enough support for management to occur.

The modern story includes the machinery and the decisions that foresters make, including the places where reasonable people can disagree. If I were helping create an exhibit, I would want visitors to experience those decisions. An exhibit could show what sustainable forestry looks like by letting people work through a choice instead of placing a wall of text about sustainability at the end of the room. It could also show the discussion between classical silviculture and ecological silviculture, with ecologically oriented approaches being taught more often and adopted by more foresters on the ground. How do we let someone see the consequences of a choice that may take 20, 50, or 100 years to become clear?

One possibility begins with a forest that many visitors would probably find beautiful. Put someone in an old-growth stand and they may decide that it should remain as it is, which may be entirely appropriate. Put the same person in a parkland stand with a completely open understory and they may reach the same conclusion based on how it looks. An open understory can feel peaceful and healthy. In a northern hardwood stand, however, the ability to see straight through the understory may mean that deer are completely mowing it over and that the next generation of trees is missing.

That creates a better interpretive question than a wall of text: What does a good, healthy forest actually look like, and how can appearance mislead us? A visitor could be asked to choose which trees remain, whether to open the canopy, how to respond to deer browse, and what kind of regeneration to encourage. Then the exhibit could move time forward and let the visitor see what followed from those decisions. It could also show how our ideas changed across the 19th, 20th, and early 21st centuries, since the standards foresters hold themselves to have changed along with what we understand about forest structure, regeneration, habitat, and long-term management.

Old growth would need to be handled carefully in an exhibit like that. We should be able to speak to the merits of old forests while also creating a defensible space for management to operate alongside them. A healthy landscape can include old growth, working forests, young forest conditions, and places where active intervention is needed to address regeneration or other problems. Visitors should be able to see the tradeoffs without being pushed toward the comfortable assumption that leaving every stand visually unchanged will always produce the best outcome.

I am developing a small game around some of these ideas called *Stand*. It is still in development, and I do not know exactly where it will end up, but the basic idea is to let someone make management choices and see what the outcomes can look like. A museum could use the same idea. Modern forestry becomes easier to understand when people can make a decision, live with the result, and try to work out why the stand responded the way it did.

I began this line of thinking with a canceled interview and an open afternoon. I ended up following numbered posts along an interpretive trail, learning more about the history of logging in the place where I happened to be. That was a good use of a rainy-day alternative plan, and I will keep making these museum stops whenever I can. I like the history, and I like seeing the different ways communities have pooled resources to preserve it.

I also want the story to keep going. The log jams, horse teams, camps, and early skidders belong in these museums, while the foresters, operators, technologies, standards, and debates of the present deserve room beside them. Forestry is a true field that still exists. It may be struggling in some places, and there may be too few people interested in entering it, but those conditions are tied in part to whether people can see the profession clearly enough to imagine a future for it.

To optimize a system, you may have to press outside your comfort zone. Maybe that is what a current forestry exhibit should ask visitors to do. The forest that feels most comfortable at first glance may not be the healthiest forest, and the forestry that belongs in a museum should leave room for the profession in the present tense.
