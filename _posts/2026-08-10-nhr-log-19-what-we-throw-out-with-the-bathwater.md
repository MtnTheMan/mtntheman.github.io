When a tree comes down, some people see the stump and decide that forestry itself is unsustainable. I understand that reaction, especially as someone who loves trees, and forestry has given people plenty of bad examples to remember. But if responsible forest management gets lumped together with high grading and overharvesting, we end up throwing the baby out with the bathwater.

Forestry has earned some of the distrust. Its public history is full of black-and-white photographs of people with crosscut saws beside massive trees in the 1800s, and irresponsible forestry still happens. A landowner can still have the best and most valuable stems taken from a stand while the poor-quality trees are left behind, which may bring in money now while making the future forest worse. Calling that work forestry does not excuse it.

For me, the baby is our ability to improve forests through active management, along with the human benefits that can come from using them responsibly. Across the northern hardwoods, many stands have already been cut, high-graded, cleared, or otherwise moved away from their earlier composition. The choice in those places is rarely between active management and some made-up virgin state that has been waiting untouched for us. Sometimes leaving a forest alone is appropriate, but doing nothing does not automatically undo the direction set by earlier land use.

A forester may consult old township surveys to learn which species and sizes were recorded as boundary trees, using those records as clues about the forest that existed before. That history can inform the work without becoming an exact recipe for recreating the past. The forester still has to read the current stand, understand what the tree species can and cannot do, consider the landowner's goals, and decide what kind of forest can reasonably be fostered from the conditions that exist now. Forestry is both an art and a science, and that combination leaves little room for a one-size-fits-all silvicultural system.

One of the uncomfortable parts of this discussion is that responsible management does not always look gentle. A relatively light harvest can be high grading if it removes the best stems and leaves the stand with poor prospects, while an aggressive cut can be aimed at creating enough light for the next cohort of trees. Counting stumps will not tell you which one happened. You have to know what was removed, what was retained, what is expected to regenerate, and whether anyone is thinking about the stand ten, twenty, or fifty years from now.

A good forester also has to tell a landowner things they may not want to hear. Landowner goals shape a management plan, but those goals can conflict with one another or with the biology of the trees. A forester who cares about the future success of the stand may discourage an owner from overharvesting, even when taking more valuable timber would produce a larger check today. The relationship may last for decades, with ten years or more between entries, so professional reputation and good word of mouth have to rest on what remains after the harvest.

What I want from a skeptical reader is fairly modest: reserve judgment when you see trees being cut. That pause is not automatic approval. It is time to ask what the cut is meant to accomplish and what will grow there afterward. Everything that lives will die, and trees are no different, so every forest will experience disturbance at some level, whether it comes from a harvest, wind, insects, disease, or the ordinary death of individual trees. The visible disturbance is one moment in a much longer process.

Forestry is extractive in the immediate sense that trees leave the woods, although wood is also renewable. It literally grows on trees. With successful regeneration, a forest can continue producing wood, habitat, employment, and other benefits on a timeline that is very different from fossil resources. Losing responsible forestry would mean losing part of our capacity to improve forests while receiving those benefits.

I do not want the baby-and-bathwater argument to become cover for every cut or every person holding a forestry degree. I want us to be more precise about what we are looking at. High grading and management that sacrifices the future stand deserve criticism. If we throw away active forest management along with those practices, though, we also throw away one of the tools available to leave healthier forests behind.
