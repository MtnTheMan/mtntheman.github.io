Another Northern Hardwood blog post idea I had was to map the different pressures that Northern Hardwood forests face across the extent of their range. This idea is similar to the [previous post about mapping economic pressures and the health of forest-products markets](https://www.mtntheman.com/northern-hardwoods/research/travel/2026/09/18/nhr-log-36-mapping-market-health.html), although I want the Northern Hardwood Pressure Atlas to stay focused on ecological and biophysical aspects of the resource. Market weaknesses, ownership patterns, institutional capacity, and other management constraints belong in adjoining layers and, eventually, in the agent-based model we are developing to describe whether different actors are aware of resiliency concerns and able to respond to them.

The first Pressure Atlas would establish a present-day baseline using the best information sources we have available. It could show where different pressures are present, where they appear to be most intense, and where several of them overlap across the Northern Hardwood Belt. Those pressures could include deer and moose browse, invasive plants such as buckthorn, pests and pathogens such as emerald ash borer or cherry leaf spot, drought and other climate-related stress, and additional disturbances that affect the health and vitality of Northern Hardwood forests.

Some of the variables I originally placed in that list, including current tree mortality, overmature stands, and degraded forest conditions, may belong somewhere else in the model. They describe the condition of the forest or its sensitivity to a pressure rather than the pressure itself. Keeping those distinctions visible would allow the Atlas to say something defensible about the location and magnitude of threats without claiming that the map has directly measured resilience.

I still want the larger project to produce a fuller picture of resiliency within a region or an explicit spatial unit of some sort. Pressure, sensitivity, potential impact, vulnerability, and adaptive capacity would remain separate but connected layers. When several pressure layers accumulate in the same place, the Atlas could demonstrate the amount of difficulty we might have in managing Northern Hardwood stands in the near future and why the response will probably have to be multifaceted, drawing from several different categories of management.

## What other cumulative-pressure maps have done

The examples I found during a quick literature review approach cumulative-pressure mapping from several directions, and each one raises a design question for the Northern Hardwood Pressure Atlas.

The terrestrial Human Footprint combines remotely sensed and survey-based information about built environments, population density, electrical infrastructure, croplands, pasturelands, roads, railways, and navigable waterways. Each input is standardized to a common scale and combined into a cumulative measure of human pressure. The authors checked the resulting map against visually interpreted pressure data from 3,114 sample plots, giving us an example of how a composite map can be evaluated using an independent source rather than accepted because the layers appear reasonable when they are placed together ([Venter et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1038/sdata.2016.67)).

The Forest Landscape Integrity Index moves that type of analysis into forests by combining observed pressures, pressures inferred from proximity and edge effects, and lost forest connectivity. Its separation of directly mapped and inferred pressure is particularly relevant here. Some disturbances can be measured through inventories, monitoring, or remote sensing, while others have to be estimated from their relationships with roads, infrastructure, forest edges, host availability, or nearby observations. The index provides a composite value, but the value is built from components that can still be examined separately ([Grantham et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-19493-3)).

Marine cumulative-impact mapping introduces another piece that I think we need. Halpern and colleagues combined the intensity of 17 anthropogenic drivers with the vulnerability of 20 marine ecosystem types. Their method treats the presence of a pressure and its potential effect on a particular ecosystem as different pieces of information. An identical magnitude of disturbance can produce different estimated impacts depending on what is exposed to it ([Halpern et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1149345)).

The Forest Service's National Insect and Disease Risk Map brings this general approach closer to forestry. Its framework identifies individual insects and diseases, links them to host species, ranks and weights criteria related to susceptibility and vulnerability, and converts the results into expected basal-area loss over a 15-year period. The national assessment assembled 186 agent-specific models while allowing regional data and expert knowledge to inform them. That approach avoids treating all pests and pathogens as one interchangeable pressure. Each agent is considered in relation to its hosts and the forest conditions that affect the likelihood of mortality ([Krist et al. 2010](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/37098)).

A broader Forest Service vulnerability framework divides assessments into exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity. Although the examples in the guide emphasize climate vulnerability, the authors describe the structure as applicable to insects, disease, invasive species, and drought ([Elmer et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.2737/RMRS-RN-96)). That division fits the problem I am trying to work through. Deer density or measured browse can represent exposure. The abundance of preferred browse species and the existing condition of regeneration can affect sensitivity. Management options and the forest's ability to accommodate disturbance contribute to adaptive capacity.

A recent assessment of salmon watersheds in coastal British Columbia mapped more than 30 pressures identified through spatial data and the knowledge of Indigenous and non-Indigenous experts. The authors calculated cumulative impacts for salmon and other species associated with the larger "salmon forest." Some hotspots changed when the assessment moved from salmon alone to the broader group of interdependent species, demonstrating how strongly a result can depend on the ecological target selected for analysis ([Finn et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1139/facets-2025-0082)).

All of these examples return us to the same set of choices. We have to define the ecological resource being assessed, separate pressure from sensitivity and observed condition, decide how each variable will be transformed and weighted, account for uneven data quality, and preserve the individual layers underneath any composite score. Those choices are part of the model, even if they are less visually interesting than the finished map.

## What belongs in the first Pressure Atlas

For ungulate pressure, we might begin with deer and moose population density, observed browse intensity, or some combination of those measurements. Population density could help estimate the amount of browse pressure occurring in different parts of the range, but that relationship would have to be checked against the availability of regeneration and evidence of browse on the ground. An identical deer-density value may have different implications depending on species composition, the regeneration layer, alternative forage, snow conditions, and other local factors.

Invasive plants would require a list of the most prescient species affecting what a forester can and cannot do across the Northern Hardwood range. Buckthorn is one example, but the complete list would need to reflect regional differences and the availability of defensible occurrence or abundance data. Presence records, modeled habitat suitability, observed cover, and rate of spread describe different things. The Atlas should identify those differences rather than treating the measurements as interchangeable.

Pests and pathogens raise a similar problem. We could map where individual agents are currently prevalent, where suitable hosts occur, and where conditions appear favorable for establishment or expansion. Emerald ash borer pressure would be interpreted partly in relation to the amount of ash present. A pathogen affecting one species or part of the canopy would require a different host and sensitivity model. The Forest Service insect and disease framework gives us a precedent for maintaining separate agent models before translating them into a common outcome.

Climate pressure could include current drought exposure, temperature and precipitation departures, extreme events, or other variables that have a defensible relationship with Northern Hardwood function. Current tree mortality and degraded or overmature stand conditions could then describe observed effects or ecological sensitivity. I originally thought of all of these as pressures, but the Atlas will be easier to defend if the labels describe what each variable actually represents.

The individual maps would show what the Northern Hardwood resource is up against. A composite model could then summarize where several pressures overlap or occur at heightened magnitudes. That score still has to be interpretable, since a high value caused by severe browse pressure should remain distinguishable from one caused by invasive plants, drought, and several moderate pest concerns.

## A first mathematical structure

Variables measured in different units have to be placed on a common scale before they can be combined. Deer density, invasive-plant cover, drought indices, pest-occurrence probabilities, and pathogen prevalence cannot be averaged in their original units. For pressure <span class="math-inline">\\(k\\)</span>, spatial unit <span class="math-inline">\\(s\\)</span>, and time <span class="math-inline">\\(t\\)</span>, a threshold-based standardization could be written as:

<div class="nhr-equation" role="img" aria-label="Standardized pressure z sub k at location s and time t equals the raw pressure value minus its lower threshold, divided by the difference between upper and lower thresholds, with the result constrained between zero and one.">
\[
z_k(s,t)
=
\min
\left\{
1,
\max
\left[
0,
\frac{x_k(s,t)-L_k}{U_k-L_k}
\right]
\right\},
\qquad U_k>L_k.
\tag{1}
\]
</div>

The subscript <span class="math-inline">\\(k\\)</span> identifies one pressure, <span class="math-inline">\\(s\\)</span> identifies the spatial unit being mapped, and <span class="math-inline">\\(t\\)</span> identifies the time associated with the observation or estimate. We still have to decide whether the spatial unit should be a raster cell, hexagonal grid cell, watershed, or another unit that can be applied consistently across the Northern Hardwood Belt.

The raw value <span class="math-inline">\\(x_k(s,t)\\)</span> is the measured or modeled intensity of pressure <span class="math-inline">\\(k\\)</span> at that place and time. The lower threshold <span class="math-inline">\\(L_k\\)</span> identifies a value at or below which the pressure receives a standardized score of zero, while the upper threshold <span class="math-inline">\\(U_k\\)</span> identifies a value at or above which the pressure receives a score of one. The minimum and maximum functions clip intermediate calculations so that every standardized pressure, <span class="math-inline">\\(z_k(s,t)\\)</span>, remains between zero and one.

Those thresholds would have to come from ecological evidence, management thresholds, observed distributions, or a documented combination of those sources. Using the smallest and largest observed values in each new dataset would make the calculation easy, but it would also make comparisons across time unstable. A deer-density value could receive a different score simply because the observed range changed in a later dataset. Fixed and defensible thresholds would give the index a more consistent interpretation over time.

Equation 1 is a generic structure, and it does not assume that every pressure responds linearly between its lower and upper thresholds. Some pressures may remain fairly minor until they cross a threshold. Others may rise quickly at low levels and then level off. Each pressure-specific transformation would need its own defense rather than being forced into a linear relationship for the sake of producing one clean equation.

Once each pressure has been standardized, a simple cumulative-pressure index could be calculated as a weighted mean:

<div class="nhr-equation" role="img" aria-label="Cumulative pressure C at location s and time t equals the weighted sum of all standardized pressures divided by the sum of their nonnegative weights.">
\[
C(s,t)
=
\frac{
\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k z_k(s,t)
}{
\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k
},
\qquad
w_k\geq 0,
\qquad
\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_k>0.
\tag{2}
\]
</div>

The term <span class="math-inline">\\(C(s,t)\\)</span> is the cumulative-pressure score for spatial unit <span class="math-inline">\\(s\\)</span> at time <span class="math-inline">\\(t\\)</span>, and <span class="math-inline">\\(K\\)</span> is the number of pressure variables included. The weight <span class="math-inline">\\(w_k\\)</span> controls the relative contribution of pressure <span class="math-inline">\\(k\\)</span>. When every <span class="math-inline">\\(w_k\\)</span> equals one, Equation 2 reduces to the average of the standardized pressure scores. Because the standardized inputs range from zero to one and the weights are nonnegative, the cumulative score also remains between zero and one.

Weights in a descriptive index are model-design choices. They are different from regression coefficients and do not acquire statistical significance simply because they appear in an equation. We could begin with equal weights, derive weights from the literature, use structured expert elicitation, or estimate them by relating the pressure layers to observed forest outcomes. Each choice answers a somewhat different question, and the method should identify which one was used.

The number of variables assigned to each category presents another balancing problem. If the pest and pathogen category contains 30 individual layers while herbivory contains two, a direct sum could allow pests and pathogens to dominate the index because more datasets were available for that category. A two-stage calculation could first combine variables within each category and then combine the category scores:

<div class="nhr-equation" role="img" aria-label="Pressure category score P sub g equals the weighted sum of standardized variables assigned to category g divided by the sum of their within-category weights.">
\[
P_g(s,t)
=
\frac{
\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_g}
a_{kg}z_k(s,t)
}{
\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathcal{K}_g}a_{kg}
}.
\tag{3a}
\]
</div>

<div class="nhr-equation" role="img" aria-label="Balanced cumulative pressure equals the weighted sum of category pressure scores divided by the sum of the category weights.">
\[
C_{\mathrm{balanced}}(s,t)
=
\frac{
\displaystyle\sum_{g=1}^{G}
v_gP_g(s,t)
}{
\displaystyle\sum_{g=1}^{G}v_g
}.
\tag{3b}
\]
</div>

The subscript <span class="math-inline">\\(g\\)</span> identifies a pressure category, such as ungulate herbivory, invasive plants, pests and pathogens, or climate stress. The set <span class="math-inline">\\(\mathcal{K}_g\\)</span> contains the individual variables assigned to category <span class="math-inline">\\(g\\)</span>, while <span class="math-inline">\\(G\\)</span> is the total number of categories. The within-category weight <span class="math-inline">\\(a_{kg}\\)</span> determines the contribution of variable <span class="math-inline">\\(k\\)</span> to category score <span class="math-inline">\\(P_g(s,t)\\)</span>. The category weight <span class="math-inline">\\(v_g\\)</span> determines the contribution of category <span class="math-inline">\\(g\\)</span> to the balanced cumulative score.

This structure prevents a category from receiving more influence solely because it contains more mappable variables. Correlated inputs would still require attention. Several layers may describe nearly the same process, and including all of them could count that process several times. Correlation analysis, conceptual review, and sensitivity testing would be needed before deciding which variables belong together.

## Keeping pressure separate from potential impact

The cumulative scores in Equations 2 and 3 describe the amount of mapped pressure. They do not establish that the forest has already experienced an equivalent ecological effect. Estimating potential impact requires information about the sensitivity of the Northern Hardwood resource to each pressure:

<div class="nhr-equation" role="img" aria-label="Potential impact I at location s and time t equals the weighted sum of each standardized pressure multiplied by the forest's sensitivity to that pressure, divided by the sum of the weights.">
\[
I(s,t)
=
\frac{
\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}
w_kz_k(s,t)q_k(s,t)
}{
\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_k
}.
\tag{4}
\]
</div>

The term <span class="math-inline">\\(I(s,t)\\)</span> is a sensitivity-adjusted potential-impact score. The new term <span class="math-inline">\\(q_k(s,t)\\)</span>, also bounded between zero and one, represents the ecological sensitivity of the forest in spatial unit <span class="math-inline">\\(s\\)</span> to pressure <span class="math-inline">\\(k\\)</span> at time <span class="math-inline">\\(t\\)</span>. The product <span class="math-inline">\\(z_k(s,t)q_k(s,t)\\)</span> remains small when either the pressure or the relevant sensitivity is low.

For emerald ash borer, <span class="math-inline">\\(q_k(s,t)\\)</span> might depend partly on ash abundance and size structure. For browse pressure, it might incorporate the abundance of preferred species, the amount of exposed regeneration, and existing evidence of recruitment limitation. For drought, sensitivity might depend on species composition, soils, stand structure, and previous disturbance. Each of those sensitivity functions would need an ecological justification.

Equation 4 belongs in a connected layer rather than being relabeled as measured resilience. It estimates potential impact under a specified set of assumptions. Observed mortality, regeneration failure, or compositional change would provide outcome data against which that estimate could eventually be tested.

If an outcome can be defined and measured consistently, the weights could be replaced with empirically estimated coefficients. For a binary outcome <span class="math-inline">\\(Y(s,t)\\)</span>, such as whether regeneration failure exceeds a defined threshold, one possible model would be:

<div class="nhr-equation" role="img" aria-label="The linear predictor eta equals an intercept plus the main effects of sensitivity-adjusted pressures and all modeled pairwise pressure interactions.">
\[
\eta(s,t)
=
\beta_0
+
\sum_{k=1}^{K}
\beta_k
\left[
z_k(s,t)q_k(s,t)
\right]
+
\sum_{k<j}
\gamma_{kj}
\left[
z_k(s,t)q_k(s,t)
\right]
\left[
z_j(s,t)q_j(s,t)
\right].
\tag{5a}
\]
</div>

<div class="nhr-equation" role="img" aria-label="The probability of outcome Y equaling one is the inverse logistic transformation of the linear predictor eta.">
\[
\Pr\!\left[Y(s,t)=1\right]
=
\frac{1}{1+\exp[-\eta(s,t)]}.
\tag{5b}
\]
</div>

The linear predictor <span class="math-inline">\\(\eta(s,t)\\)</span> combines the modeled terms before they are converted to a probability. The intercept <span class="math-inline">\\(\beta_0\\)</span> describes the baseline log-odds of the outcome when the included pressure-sensitivity terms equal zero. Each coefficient <span class="math-inline">\\(\beta_k\\)</span> estimates the relationship between the outcome and the sensitivity-adjusted intensity of pressure <span class="math-inline">\\(k\\)</span>, conditional on the other variables in the model.

The coefficient <span class="math-inline">\\(\gamma_{kj}\\)</span> describes an interaction between pressures <span class="math-inline">\\(k\\)</span> and <span class="math-inline">\\(j\\)</span>. A positive value would indicate that their joint association with the outcome is greater than the contribution represented by their separate main effects on the log-odds scale. A negative value would indicate a weaker joint association. Those coefficients would need to be estimated from outcome data. In the absence of those data, they should remain scenario parameters or be omitted rather than presented as fitted ecological relationships.

For an estimated coefficient, its conventional standard error comes from the diagonal of the estimated coefficient covariance matrix:

<div class="nhr-equation" role="img" aria-label="The standard error of estimated coefficient beta sub k equals the square root of diagonal element k k in the estimated coefficient covariance matrix.">
\[
SE\!\left(\widehat{\beta}_k\right)
=
\sqrt{
\left[
\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}
\left(
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
\right)
\right]_{kk}
}.
\tag{6}
\]
</div>

The vector <span class="math-inline">\\(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\\)</span> contains the estimated coefficients, and the <span class="math-inline">\\(kk\\)</span> element of its covariance matrix is the estimated variance of <span class="math-inline">\\(\widehat{\beta}_k\\)</span>. The same calculation applies to an estimated interaction coefficient <span class="math-inline">\\(\widehat{\gamma}_{kj}\\)</span>. An approximate 95 percent confidence interval could be reported as <span class="math-inline">\\(\widehat{\beta}_k\pm1.96SE(\widehat{\beta}_k)\\)</span>, provided that the model and its asymptotic assumptions are appropriate. Neighboring map cells are unlikely to be statistically independent, so the eventual estimation procedure would need to account for spatial structure rather than treating every pixel as an unrelated observation.

## Uncertainty in the composite map

A composite score should carry uncertainty from its input layers. If the weights are treated as fixed and the standardized pressure estimates have a known or estimated covariance matrix, the sampling variance of Equation 2 can be written as:

<div class="nhr-equation" role="img" aria-label="The variance of estimated cumulative pressure equals the weight vector transposed times the pressure covariance matrix times the weight vector, divided by the squared sum of weights.">
\[
\operatorname{Var}
\left[
\widehat{C}(s,t)
\right]
=
\frac{
\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf T}
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{z}(s,t)
\mathbf{w}
}{
\left(
\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{w}
\right)^2
}.
\tag{7a}
\]
</div>

<div class="nhr-equation" role="img" aria-label="The standard error of estimated cumulative pressure equals the square root of its variance.">
\[
SE
\left[
\widehat{C}(s,t)
\right]
=
\sqrt{
\operatorname{Var}
\left[
\widehat{C}(s,t)
\right]
}.
\tag{7b}
\]
</div>

The vector <span class="math-inline">\\(\mathbf{w}\\)</span> contains the pressure weights, <span class="math-inline">\\(\mathbf{1}\\)</span> is a vector of ones, and <span class="math-inline">\\(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{z}(s,t)\\)</span> is the covariance matrix for the standardized pressure estimates at spatial unit <span class="math-inline">\\(s\\)</span> and time <span class="math-inline">\\(t\\)</span>. The diagonal elements of <span class="math-inline">\\(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{z}\\)</span> contain the variance of each pressure estimate. Its off-diagonal elements describe covariance among the estimates, preventing the uncertainty calculation from assuming that related pressure layers are independent.

Equation 7 addresses sampling uncertainty when the required covariance estimates are available and the weights and thresholds are fixed. The Atlas would also contain uncertainty from thresholds, transformations, missing data, expert weights, climate scenarios, model selection, and interactions. A simulation-based calculation is more flexible for those sources.

Let <span class="math-inline">\\(\boldsymbol{\theta}\\)</span> contain every uncertain input or parameter included in the index, including pressure estimates, thresholds, weights, sensitivity values, and any fitted coefficients. For simulation <span class="math-inline">\\(b\\)</span>, we would draw a plausible parameter vector from a joint distribution and recalculate the index:

<div class="nhr-equation" role="img" aria-label="For each simulation b from one through B, draw parameter vector theta from its joint distribution and calculate cumulative pressure using the complete index function.">
\[
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(b)}
\sim
F_{\boldsymbol{\theta}},
\qquad
C^{(b)}(s,t)
=
f
\left(
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(b)};s,t
\right),
\qquad
b=1,\ldots,B.
\tag{8}
\]
</div>

The distribution <span class="math-inline">\\(F_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\\)</span> describes what is known about the parameters and their dependence, <span class="math-inline">\\(f\\)</span> is the complete index calculation, and <span class="math-inline">\\(B\\)</span> is the number of simulations. The resulting mean and uncertainty standard deviation would be:

<div class="nhr-equation" role="img" aria-label="Mean cumulative pressure equals the sum of simulated cumulative-pressure values divided by the number of simulations B.">
\[
\overline{C}(s,t)
=
\frac{1}{B}
\sum_{b=1}^{B}
C^{(b)}(s,t).
\tag{9a}
\]
</div>

<div class="nhr-equation" role="img" aria-label="The uncertainty standard deviation equals the square root of the summed squared differences between each simulated value and the simulation mean, divided by B minus one.">
\[
u_C(s,t)
=
\sqrt{
\frac{1}{B-1}
\sum_{b=1}^{B}
\left[
C^{(b)}(s,t)-\overline{C}(s,t)
\right]^2
}.
\tag{9b}
\]
</div>

Lower and upper uncertainty bounds could be taken from the simulated quantiles:

<div class="nhr-equation" role="img" aria-label="The 90 percent uncertainty interval runs from the fifth percentile to the ninety-fifth percentile of the simulated cumulative-pressure values.">
\[
\operatorname{UI}_{90\%}(s,t)
=
\left[
Q_{0.05}
\left\{
C^{(b)}(s,t)
\right\},
Q_{0.95}
\left\{
C^{(b)}(s,t)
\right\}
\right].
\tag{9c}
\]
</div>

The term <span class="math-inline">\\(u_C(s,t)\\)</span> should be called an uncertainty standard deviation when the simulations represent uncertain assumptions and model choices. It can be interpreted as a standard error only when the simulated distribution represents repeated sampling of an estimator. Using the more accurate label keeps us from making the uncertainty appear more conventionally statistical than it is.

Stock and Micheli found that eight of nine tested assumptions or data-quality problems had a considerable influence on at least one result in their evaluation of cumulative-impact maps. The influence of those factors also varied between study regions ([Stock and Micheli 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/geb.12493)). A Northern Hardwood Pressure Atlas would therefore need to show where its results remain stable and where they depend heavily on a threshold, weight, missing layer, or model choice.

## Sensitivity analysis and balancing the variables

I would begin the sensitivity analysis by changing one parameter at a time while holding the remaining model constant. For uncertain parameter <span class="math-inline">\\(\theta_r\\)</span>, a centered finite-difference measure would be:

<div class="nhr-equation" role="img" aria-label="Local sensitivity D sub r equals the cumulative-pressure score with parameter theta sub r increased by delta minus the score with that parameter decreased by delta, divided by two delta.">
\[
D_r(s,t)
=
\frac{
C\left(s,t;\theta_r+\delta_r\right)
-
C\left(s,t;\theta_r-\delta_r\right)
}{
2\delta_r
}.
\tag{10}
\]
</div>

Here, <span class="math-inline">\\(\theta_r\\)</span> is the parameter being tested, <span class="math-inline">\\(\delta_r\\)</span> is a small but meaningful change in that parameter, and <span class="math-inline">\\(D_r(s,t)\\)</span> estimates how quickly the cumulative score changes near the selected parameter value. We could use it to examine a pressure weight, upper threshold, assumed detection probability, or another input. Because parameters use different units, the model would also need standardized changes, such as increasing each parameter by 10 percent or moving it across a specified uncertainty range, before their influence could be compared.

One-at-a-time changes are understandable, but they can miss interactions among uncertain inputs. A global variance-based sensitivity measure could use the simulations from Equation 8:

<div class="nhr-equation" role="img" aria-label="First-order sensitivity S sub r equals the variance in expected cumulative pressure associated with parameter theta sub r divided by the total variance in cumulative pressure.">
\[
S_r(s,t)
=
\frac{
\operatorname{Var}_{\theta_r}
\left[
\operatorname{E}
\left(
C(s,t)
\mid
\theta_r
\right)
\right]
}{
\operatorname{Var}
\left[
C(s,t)
\right]
}.
\tag{11}
\]
</div>

The numerator describes how much the expected cumulative score changes as <span class="math-inline">\\(\theta_r\\)</span> varies, averaged across the other uncertain inputs. The denominator is the total variance in the cumulative score. The resulting first-order sensitivity index <span class="math-inline">\\(S_r(s,t)\\)</span> estimates the share of output variance associated with parameter <span class="math-inline">\\(\theta_r\\)</span> by itself. Additional total-order measures could incorporate its interactions with other parameters.

These analyses would tell us whether a regional hotspot remains a hotspot under reasonable alternative assumptions. They would also identify cases where one uncertain coefficient, threshold, or weak dataset controls the result. The Atlas could display a cumulative-pressure estimate, its uncertainty interval, and a measure of result stability, while allowing users to open the individual layers and see why the model assigned a region its score.

## Adding time without losing the baseline

The present-day map should come first, but change over time could become a central future feature of the Atlas. Many of the disciplines contributing input layers already model how climate, invasive species, pests, pathogens, and forest conditions may change. Those projections could be placed on a common timeline while retaining their sensitivity thresholds and lower and upper uncertainty bounds.

For climate scenario <span class="math-inline">\\(c\\)</span>, the pressure index could be written as:

<div class="nhr-equation" role="img" aria-label="Future cumulative pressure for location s, time t, and climate scenario c equals the weighted sum of scenario-specific standardized pressures divided by the sum of weights.">
\[
C(s,t\mid c)
=
\frac{
\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}
w_kz_k(s,t\mid c)
}{
\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_k
}.
\tag{12}
\]
</div>

The scenario <span class="math-inline">\\(c\\)</span> defines the future assumptions used to project the ecological and biophysical pressure layers. Time <span class="math-inline">\\(t\\)</span> could be evaluated continuously or at intervals such as 10, 20, 50, and 100 years. I do not think that 50 or 100 years should be the only explicit horizon. A continuum would allow us to see when projected changes begin to separate from present conditions and how uncertainty expands farther into the future.

Management scenarios belong primarily in the sensitivity, potential-impact, and adaptive-capacity portions of the larger model. For climate scenario <span class="math-inline">\\(c\\)</span> and management regime <span class="math-inline">\\(m\\)</span>, a future impact layer could be written as:

<div class="nhr-equation" role="img" aria-label="Future potential impact for climate scenario c and management regime m equals the weighted sum of scenario-specific pressures multiplied by management- and climate-dependent sensitivity, divided by the sum of weights.">
\[
I(s,t\mid c,m)
=
\frac{
\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}
w_k
z_k(s,t\mid c)
q_k(s,t\mid c,m)
}{
\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_k
}.
\tag{13}
\]
</div>

In this version, climate scenario <span class="math-inline">\\(c\\)</span> affects projected pressure and forest conditions, while management regime <span class="math-inline">\\(m\\)</span> affects the sensitivity term <span class="math-inline">\\(q_k(s,t\mid c,m)\\)</span>. A business-as-usual regime could preserve the management trajectories we currently see among the actors in our study. A proactive regime could represent greater use of applied silvicultural practices intended to reduce sensitivity or improve the forest's capacity to respond.

The difference between two management scenarios could then be summarized as:

<div class="nhr-equation" role="img" aria-label="Delta I equals potential impact under proactive management minus potential impact under business-as-usual management for the same place, time, and climate scenario.">
\[
\Delta I(s,t\mid c)
=

\left(

\right)
-

\left(
s,t\mid c,m_{\mathrm{BAU}}
\right).
\tag{14}
\]
</div>

A negative value of <span class="math-inline">\\(\Delta I(s,t\mid c)\\)</span> would indicate lower modeled potential impact under the proactive scenario than under business as usual. The comparison would still depend on the assumed relationship between management and ecological sensitivity. The agent-based model could eventually add ownership categories, awareness, institutional constraints, market conditions, and the probability that an actor adopts or maintains a particular practice.

## Equipping foresters to describe what they are up against

I think a Pressure Atlas would be good for demonstrating the amount of difficulty we may face in managing Northern Hardwood stands in the near future. The literature search for this post turned up several partial precedents, but I did not find a range-wide resource that allows foresters, administrators, or land managers to describe all of these Northern Hardwood pressures together. That claim still needs a more exhaustive search before I would state it categorically.

The Atlas could be shared first with foresters, who could translate it into something useful for administrators and policymakers. If there is a comparative need to address an issue, foresters should have some way to describe the resources they manage, the pressures affecting those resources, and the mitigation problems that may be coming up for them. A region facing intense browse, expanding invasive plants, a vulnerable host composition, and increasing climate stress will require a different conversation than a region where one pressure dominates.

A composite score can start that comparison, but people should always be able to return to the underlying variables. They need to know which pressures created the score, how certain the estimate is, and what combination of management responses could change the projected outcome.

For now, I want the first version of the Northern Hardwood Pressure Atlas to establish the baseline and show what is at play. Later versions could chart how pressures and forest sensitivities change over time, then compare how the forest might fare under business-as-usual and more proactive management regimes. If the Atlas works, foresters will be better equipped to describe which pressures occur where, how strongly they overlap, and why maintaining resilience in some parts of the Northern Hardwood Belt may require several management responses working together.
