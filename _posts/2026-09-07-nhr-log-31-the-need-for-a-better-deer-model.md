Getting down to my last little blog-post ideas here, this is one I keep returning to because it is intrinsic to the whole study. I have been calling it “The Need for a Better Deer Model,” and the idea grew out of something that feels pretty weird once you look closely at it. Foresters, especially in Michigan, share a fairly universal impression that there are too many deer on the landscape and that browsing is one of the major factors contributing to regeneration failure. Yet the ways we count deer change across state lines, and we do not have a current population-density layer that has been sampled or modeled consistently across the Northern Hardwood Range.

Michigan is why my mind went to deer first. We are so hyper-focused on deer here because deer are such a massive problem for northern hardwoods specifically in Michigan. Heavy browse changes how these forests regenerate, and it is especially hard on many of the species foresters hope to carry into the next stand. Sugar maple, yellow birch, and other valuable merchantable species can have a notoriously difficult time getting above browse height. They are browsed over and over until they disappear from the regeneration layer, or until the species that deer avoid gain the advantage. Deer are capable of mowing things down like a lawnmower.

That explanation is so familiar in Michigan that it is easy to carry it everywhere. During this project, though, that assumption did not continue to hold across the whole Northern Hardwood Belt. It was very prescient in a lot of places, but in some regions regeneration occurs just fine with no significant deer browse pressure. The trip turned what sounded like a universal explanation into a regional question: where are deer actually exerting enough pressure to change regeneration, and where are we repeating the explanation because it is the problem we know best?

## The problem changes across the range

In warmer parts of the Northern Hardwood Belt, and in some of the more densely human-populated areas we visited, deer browse was one of the largest and most commonly used explanations for poor regeneration. That is still a field impression, however, and I would want a consistent map before treating it as a settled geographic relationship. One national analysis using deer-density classes from around 2003 actually found lower deer density with increasing residential development within the regions it studied, a useful complication to any simple suburban-deer story ([Hanberry 2021](https://doi.org/10.1002/ece3.8084)).

There are also many different things that can contribute to a lack of forest health. A pest, pathogen, leaf disease, invasive plant, past harvest, or difficult site condition may weaken a stand or constrain its response to disturbance. Each tree species also arrives with its own litany of pests and pathogens to consider. Deer feel different because one browsing population can filter the regeneration of several preferred species at once. That makes deer look like a single source that is omnipresent across the Northern Hardwood Belt, when in actuality they are probably one part of a larger matrix of concerns affecting stand health and the ability of a stand to respond after disturbance.

The distinction has consequences for how we compare management outcomes. I have written elsewhere about the [combinations of practices managers use](https://www.mtntheman.com/northern-hardwoods/research/travel/2026/08/03/nhr-log-18-practice-bundles-and-the-problem-of-millions-of-forest-managers.html) and [the gray area involved in modeling them](https://www.mtntheman.com/northern-hardwoods/research/travel/2026/08/17/nhr-log-22-practice-bundles-part-two-modeling-the-gray-area.html). If we compare regeneration across the range without a comparable wildlife layer, we cannot tell whether similar silviculture produced different outcomes, or whether the same treatment was operating under very different browse pressure. A range-wide ungulate model would give us a way to test how much of the difference deer and moose can independently explain.

## The maps and models we already have

The strange part is that this idea does not require inventing deer population modeling from scratch. Pieces of it already exist, although they answer different questions and work at different scales.

There have been national deer-density maps. Hanberry and Hanberry digitized thematic maps for 1982 and roughly 2003 that had been assembled largely from state-agency estimates, harvest information, and deer surveys ([Hanberry and Hanberry 2020](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.8262)). Those maps are useful historical snapshots, but they also inherit differences in the way states produced the original estimates. A separate Forest Service effort used regionwide forest inventory plots to map the probability of moderate or high ungulate browse impacts across the Midwest and Northeast ([McWilliams et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.2737/NRS-GTR-182)). That gets closer to the ecological pressure I care about, although a browse-impact map and an animal-density map are not the same thing.

States have developed much more detailed systems inside their own boundaries. Wisconsin, for example, estimates deer populations by management unit using its Sex-Age-Kill framework, harvest and age data, fawn-to-doe ratios informed by public observation surveys and Snapshot Wisconsin cameras, and spatial and temporal smoothing ([Wisconsin DNR 2024](https://www.wistatedocuments.org/digital/api/collection/p267601coll4/id/35536/download)). The estimate draws on several sources, but its units and assumptions belong to Wisconsin’s management system.

Other studies show how independent observations can strengthen those estimates. Researchers in the Adirondack Park tied winter aerial surveys to density-surface modeling to produce a spatially explicit estimate across a large northern forest landscape ([Hinton et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0273707)). In Wisconsin, researchers combined broad but coarse harvest records with fine-resolution citizen-science camera-trap data in integrated species-distribution models ([Gilbert et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biocon.2021.109147)). Pellet-group counts can also produce landscape-level density estimates when the sampling and corrections are handled carefully ([deCalesta 2013](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/43285)). These are different ways of getting beyond a simple map of harvested deer.

Harvest is useful information, but it is also a record of people, access, regulations, effort, and deer. More harvest in one rural area does not automatically mean more deer there than in a subdivision where hunting access is limited. A survey of Midwestern states found substantial differences in the spatial resolution, structure, and collection of harvest data across jurisdictions ([Brandell et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fevo.2022.943411)). The missing map therefore looks as much like an institutional problem as a technical one. Each state is charged with measuring and managing its own herd, and each has built a system around its own laws, traditions, landscape, and available data. Putting the outputs next to each other does not make them comparable.

## Building a range-wide ungulate layer

The model I am imagining would resemble a human population-density grid: one common spatial framework across the Northern Hardwood Range, perhaps at a kilometer-scale resolution, showing estimated density and uncertainty for white-tailed deer and, where they overlap, moose and other large browsers. It would need to cover the Canadian portion of the range as well as the United States. Most of all, it would need data beyond traditional harvest metrics.

That could mean reconciling harvest and hunter-effort records with camera networks, aerial or thermal surveys, pellet counts, road mortality, field observations, recruitment measures, and direct indicators of browse. Habitat, forest cover, agriculture, roads, snow, winter severity, and human development could help the model interpolate between sampled places. Some sources measure abundance, some measure animal use, and some measure the effect on plants, so the model would have to keep those observation processes separate. Otherwise, we would risk using damaged regeneration to predict deer density and then turning around and using the predicted density to explain the same damage.

There are geospatial methods for doing this. A finished layer might use spatial smoothing, kriging, hierarchical models, or other tools that can carry information across gaps while retaining the uncertainty associated with each proxy. Artificial intelligence could help with the tedious work of reconciling formats and finding relationships among many sources. It cannot make the assumptions or weak points of those proxies disappear. The best possible model would have to show where it is well supported, where it is leaning heavily on indirect information, and where states or provinces are effectively speaking different measurement languages.

In that sense, the technical work may be relatively easy compared with the institutional coordination. The difficult part is agreeing on what should be comparable, preserving useful local information, and being honest about what every estimate means. States and provinces could keep their own management methods while translating them into a shared analytical layer.

I want that layer so we can put it beside regeneration outcomes across the Northern Hardwood Belt. Deer may be a huge issue in one region and much less of an issue elsewhere, while moose account for part of the browsing signal near the northern edge. Places with similar estimated deer density may still have different regeneration outcomes because winter severity, forage, disturbance history, or stand conditions change the effect. Those differences would let us independently test how strongly ungulates shape regeneration instead of letting the most familiar regional explanation stand in for the whole range.

Michigan’s deer problem has shaped how many of us see northern hardwood regeneration. The trip made me less willing to assume that the Michigan version of the problem is universal. A better deer model would show how far that explanation travels before it begins to weaken, and where we need to look harder at the rest of the matrix.
