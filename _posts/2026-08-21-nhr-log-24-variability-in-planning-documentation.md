Whenever I think about managing the northern hardwood resource, the work looks very similar across the board, even though the amount of documentation sitting behind that work can change enormously. A manager working for the U.S. Forest Service and a forester working in private procurement may operate on different sets of lands, but both are still making decisions about what gets cut, what stays, what a residual stand should look like, and what they expect to happen after the entry. One of them may have a massive amount of documentation and data informing the operation, along with multiple approvals and a lot of bureaucracy, while the other may have almost none, and the difference in the amount of things they write down is just large.

Across the northern hardwood range, that documentation can include silvicultural guides, prescriptions, best management practice resources, long-term forest management plans, harvest plans, quotas, operational plans, bidding materials, contracts, monitoring records, or a tally of the volume of wood leaving a lot. These records come in different formats and carry very different levels of detail. Even within the same state government, county system, company, or other organization, the planning process can change depending on the land, the manager, the program, and who needs to approve the work.

I came at the lower end of this question in [NHR Log 12: Good Forestry Without 50 Pages](https://www.mtntheman.com/northern-hardwoods/research/travel/2026/07/20/nhr-log-12-good-forestry-without-50-pages.html). That post asked whether good forestry could still happen when the written documentation was sparse, and I argued that a lot of forestry knowledge can live in tree marking, field judgment, apprenticeship, and the conversations surrounding an operation. Here, I am more interested in the variability itself. A thick file and a thin file may reflect very different systems of governance and accountability, while telling us less than we might expect about the amount of forestry knowledge being packed into the decisions on the ground.

We expected to encounter this broad variability when we designed the interviews for the Northern Hardwoods Resilience Project, which is one reason the project relies on individual conversations with foresters. If we depended entirely on written sources to understand how northern hardwoods are being managed, the organizations that document the most would also become the organizations we could describe most completely. Managers working with very little formal documentation would be much harder to see, regardless of what they know or how carefully they work. By talking with foresters across public agencies, industry, consulting, procurement, and other settings, we can ask what they are doing on the ground, what outcomes look like to them, how they think things are going, and what challenges they are up against without first requiring them to have written a source that explains the whole system for us.

I do not think there is one proper amount of documentation that a forester needs to have. There can be too little, and there can also be so much that the planning process begins to take time and resources away from the work it was supposed to support, but I do not think there is a range-wide Goldilocks number of pages. What counts as sufficient depends on the governance structure, the ownership, the acreage, the turnover among managers, the time horizon of the decisions, the resources available to create the record, and the people to whom the manager is accountable.

A public forester may work within a stringent set of planning standards, best management practices, public consultation requirements, bids, contracts, and internal approval processes meant to create a sense of responsibility around the whole operation. A procurement forester may work from an operations plan, mark or otherwise direct the harvest, and record the volume of wood leaving the property. Both foresters may be drawing on institutional knowledge, formal forestry education, and years of experience. Their paper trails still look almost nothing alike because the systems underneath them ask for different things.

A large industrial forest owner benefits from plans that communicate across multiple levels of the organization and provide confidence to internal partners, financial decision-makers, shareholders, or anyone else who needs to understand how the organization is operating. A small private landowner may have ten acres and a fairly clear idea in their head of what they want the forest to look like. They might want something resembling a park landscape with a relatively open understory, and they may accept conditions that a public agency managing for broader ecological goals would view differently. That landowner can hire a consulting forester and obtain a plan, but the value of a long and expensive planning process depends on what they need, what resources they have, and what decisions are actually in front of them.

Documentation becomes especially useful whenever stewardship has to pass from one person to another, because forests inherently exist beyond the horizons of a single human career. Forests will outlast a single steward's role on that landscape. State forests and national forests will see plenty of turnover over the decades, and private land also changes hands among landowners, heirs, foresters, and operators. Good recording and good note-taking give the next person a way to understand what past actions were, how they were executed, what the earlier steward was trying to accomplish, and whether the stand responded as expected. That record can carry good stewardship practices much further into the future than depending on good-faith actors in the present moment alone.

Extensive recording also enables science, since forestry is a science-based and data-driven field, and the scientific method depends on writing down what we are doing so that we can understand what was done, what worked, and what did not work. Many foresters would probably rather claim that forestry is more science than art, although every stand is a little different, which is where the art comes in. The art-and-science discussion can wait for another post; here, the practical limitation is that undocumented practice is much harder to compare, evaluate, repeat, or correct, even when the original work was thoughtful.

Public ownership adds another reason to write more down, which I think of as a kind of silvicultural fiduciary responsibility: if a forester is acting on behalf of the public, then the agency should be able to explain to taxpayers and other stakeholders what actions are being taken, why they are being taken, and how those actions relate to the future condition of the forest. Producing a long-term plan, consulting with the public, recording a decision, or documenting compliance may be an appropriate use of time in that setting because the public trust creates a wider field of accountability.

A consulting forester working with one private landowner has fewer stakeholders to answer to and can often establish priorities through direct conversation. The landowner may need help deciding what to do within an immediate timeline, what the property can support, what the work will cost, and how much revenue the wood products might provide. They do not automatically need a 100-year plan, or even a 20-year plan, to make that decision responsibly. A lighter paper trail in that context can reflect limited time, money, energy, and resources rather than a lack of willingness to be a good steward.

Those costs are where useful documentation can cross into too much documentation. The threshold arrives when producing, reviewing, and approving the record decreases efficiency on the ground enough to weaken the forestry the record was supposed to support. Where that threshold sits will still depend on the institution. A public agency may reasonably devote more staff time to planning and consultation because it carries a wider obligation to justify its decisions, while a private forester may need to spend that same time walking the stand, talking with the landowner, marking trees, overseeing the operation, or responding to a problem that cannot wait for another round of paperwork.

The health of the forest is probably what matters most as the end result. We should be asking how sustainable the practices being executed on the ground are, whether they are being conducted responsibly, and what they are likely to produce 20, 30, or 100 years from now. Immediate profits are one part of that management calculation, but the future condition of the forest is where the consequences of forestry practice or malpractice will eventually show up. The amount of documentation may be correlated with the outcomes an agency, company, or landowner wants, especially when a written plan creates continuity and supports monitoring, but the number of pages alone cannot determine whether the forest is being managed well.

In the interviews, we keep the discussion outcomes-oriented while also keeping a finger on the pulse of what proactive management looks like. We ask foresters how they define success, how they think the forest is responding, and what problems are front of mind. A good forester will usually be pretty honest about the challenges they face. Deer, invasive species, pests and pathogens, regeneration problems, markets, and operational constraints are common enough that their presence in a conversation can tell us something about how actively a manager is thinking through forest health. The absence of those problems from a conversation cannot prove that they are being ignored, although it can still be informative when issues that are common in a place never seem to enter the manager's account of the work.

Foresters also have to balance ecological needs with financial realities. Stewardship may be at the front of a forester's mind, and dollar signs still have to be somewhere in the calculation because harvesting, monitoring, restoration, and follow-up work all cost money. In my conversations with foresters, finding that balance is a common part of land stewardship rather than evidence that either the ecology or the economics has been abandoned. Forward-thinking, science-informed, data-disciplined forestry has to work within the resources available while still looking far enough ahead to understand what today's decisions are likely to leave behind.

Comparing foresters by the amount of paper they produce would give us a very clean ranking and probably the wrong one. It would favor the systems that are easiest to see in writing while discounting institutional knowledge and professional judgment in less-documented settings. It could also miss the possibility that a highly documented system has become too cumbersome to operate efficiently. Evaluating variability in planning documentation requires us to ask whether the record fits the work it needs to do: whether it can help pass stewardship between people, enable scientific learning, satisfy the obligations attached to the land, and still leave enough capacity to practice forestry on the ground.

There is no universal rule for how much documentation accomplishes that balance. The useful amount changes with who owns the land, who answers for it, who will manage it next, and what resources are available now. The forest is where the results will eventually show up, while the record helps us understand how they got there and gives the next steward something more durable than good intentions to work from.
