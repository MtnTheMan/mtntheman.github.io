One thing I’ve started paying attention to as I go through these interviews is what never comes up. Many times, managers simply do not mention things that are not concerning to them, or things that they are not currently dealing with, even when those same issues are major factors influencing another manager’s decisions somewhere else in the northern hardwood range. After enough interviews, the absence of gripes starts to look like a piece of evidence in itself. We can learn something from what people do not say in much the same way that we learn from what they do say, as long as we know what is missing and do some work to confirm why it is missing.

Deer browse is probably the clearest example. In areas with low deer populations, managers may never mention that deer are around. When they think about forest management, they are thinking about their markets, working with their operators, or regeneration that is happening on its own naturally. Deer browse is not a front-of-mind matter for them. Since these interviews were fairly exhaustive and the interview manual eventually asked about many of these concerns directly, that omission became more informative when a manager later confirmed that browse simply did not have much influence on their stands.

I began seeing the same thing across many interviews with people who felt like they were in a really great position with their northern hardwood stands, for whatever reason it might have been. I kept trying to imagine why things were going so well for them, and many times it turned out that they simply lacked some of the difficulties that managers were facing elsewhere. I might be sitting physically at a mill with someone who had complete control over a given market, or at least over one point in that market, and markets would barely enter the discussion. That person did not describe the same nightmare of trying to move wood that I would hear from another forester, sometimes in the same region and sometimes somewhere else in the forest belt.

When markets are working, when different products can be sold and low-value material can actually be moved, people may barely talk about economics. The financial side of forestry is supporting the work well enough that the manager has room to make different silvicultural choices. Bad markets stay front of mind for the opposite reason: a forester may know what kind of treatment would benefit the stand and still have no feasible way to carry it out. Knowing what good forestry should look like does not solve the problem of having nowhere to send the wood.

Managers whose usual problems have receded can practice sustainable forestry more like it was taught in a textbook. They can apply more silvicultural concepts because they can sell different wood products, they do not face as much pressure to remove the most valuable material, regeneration may come naturally, and disease may not be taking certain species out of the stand. It becomes easier to practice forestry with ecological and financial needs in mind at the same time, which is probably as close to a perfect world as forestry gets.

I wrote in an earlier post about [the invasives that are coming, the pests that are coming, and the pathogens that are coming](https://www.mtntheman.com/northern-hardwoods/research/travel/2026/08/31/nhr-log-28-the-invasives-are-coming-the-pests-are-coming-the-pathogens-are-coming.html). Those burdens are not distributed evenly across the northern hardwood range, any more than deer browse or markets are. Their absence can give a manager far more flexibility, while the presence of even one major concern can limit an otherwise good silvicultural prescription. Trying to find those middling territories across the range, where some problems have receded while others remain, has been an interesting part of interpreting all these interviews.

The financial side also cannot be separated from who owns the forest. Private landowners control much of the northern hardwood resource, especially in the United States, and financially driven decision making is a common part of managing those lands. A forester still has to provide revenue for a mill, a landowner, or whoever needs wood out of a forest. When the economics cooperate, managers have more room to do work that is responsible to the environment, their employees, and the people who own or purchase the wood. When the economics do not cooperate, severe constraints can keep a thoughtful and skilled forester from carrying out the forestry they were taught.

There are other possible explanations for silence, though, and some of them are less comfortable. A glaring reason someone might omit information is that they are not practicing forestry in an ethical manner and do not want to talk about it in an interview with an academic. A manager might also have a planning method with a blind spot around a forest health or regeneration concern. If they do not have a firm plan for addressing the concern, they may rather pass up the opportunity to discuss it, particularly when they still need to produce revenue and keep work moving. In that case, the absence of information does not tell us that the concern is absent from the forest. It might tell us that the organization does not have the readiness, planning process, or tools in its toolbelt to respond.

I want to be careful here, since it would be easy to treat the ability to practice textbook forestry as a measure of an individual forester’s skill or ethics. Many managers are working under constraints that they cannot remove by being more thoughtful. Still, there are cases where an omission may indicate that someone is not preparing for an issue on the horizon, or is not taking an active management approach to something already occurring in the stand. Those situations require more direct questioning before I can tell the difference between someone who is doing well, someone who is working within a difficult set of limits, and someone whose planning has left a concern unaddressed.

The interview manual helped with that distinction by returning directly to concerns that a manager had not raised on their own. If deer browse never arose spontaneously, but the manager later said that it carried little importance in their stands, then the earlier omission gained some analytical weight. The same could happen with markets, invasive species, pests, pathogens, or regeneration. Even then, I think the lack of concern needs to be backed up with empirical evidence when that evidence is available. An omission can guide the analysis, but it cannot carry the analysis by itself.

Across all of these interviews, there was what I first wanted to call a dearth, although cornucopia is probably the better word, of issues that can affect northern hardwood forests and the species that carry their compositions. There may be many more in the future. Managers who spend time thinking about what happens if a species is no longer viable on the landscape are going to be more ready to respond than managers who have never included that possibility in their planning. Simply failing to think about an issue does not mean that it will never come to you.

So when a familiar concern is missing from an interview, I do not want to fill that silence automatically. Sometimes the manager is in a good position and really is free of a problem that shapes forestry elsewhere. Sometimes the issue is present but has not entered the planning process, or the manager and their organization are not ready to respond to it. Direct questions, comparisons across interviews, and evidence from the forest can help tell those situations apart.

What people do not say can show us where managers have room to practice good forestry and where another question still needs to be asked. If an issue is already emerging in a stand, managers need to think about how they will pass off a good northern hardwood resource to the next generation of foresters and landowners who will have to carry that torch. These issues do not magically stop happening, and they do not magically stop emerging.
