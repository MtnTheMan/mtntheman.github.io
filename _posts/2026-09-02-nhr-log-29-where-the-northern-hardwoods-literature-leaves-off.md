*Part 4 of the NHR Literature Series: Academic Literature and the State of Management*

The last category of works that has really contributed to the Northern Hardwoods Resilience Project is academic literature: journal articles, government publications, conference proceedings, academic white papers, and the gray literature that has described what is happening across the northern hardwood resource. Work by people like Mike Walters, Christel Kern, Laura Kenefic, Ralph Nyland, and many others gave this project a scientific starting point. For this fourth post about the literature I think of as spiritual ancestors to NHR, I want to focus on the portion that led most directly into our State of Management work: previous attempts to understand northern hardwood managers, silvicultural practice, and the broader state of management across the resource.

The first three posts in this series dealt with [silviculture textbooks](https://www.mtntheman.com/northern-hardwoods/research/travel/2026/07/08/nhr-log-7-the-silviculture-textbooks-behind-nhr.html), [narrative books](https://www.mtntheman.com/northern-hardwoods/research/travel/2026/08/19/nhr-log-23-the-narrative-books-behind-nhr.html), and [regional guidebooks](https://www.mtntheman.com/northern-hardwoods/research/travel/2026/08/26/nhr-log-26-a-regional-way-of-seeing-the-forest.html). Those works gave us professional language, historical context, and regional ways of seeing forests. Academic literature records something else. It tells us what previous generations of researchers were able to measure, what questions they considered answerable, and which parts of management were visible through the data and methods they had available.

It would be convenient if that literature added up to a clean account of the best silvicultural practice and how foresters should apply it. I think those of us in academia sometimes act as though such a practice must exist, and that if we collect enough evidence, we will eventually be able to identify it and broadly recommend it. In reality, it is not clean like that. People take bits and pieces of silvicultural practice that might work best for them and apply those pieces in whatever way makes the most sense within the forest, market, ownership, organization, and regulatory system where they are working.

The academic record contains plenty of recommended practices, but it also contains a long and somewhat uneven history of how those practices became accepted, taught, modified, misapplied, and sometimes abandoned. That history is where the State of Management work really begins.

## How a practice becomes accepted

In the Lake States, part of that history begins with the long-term cutting studies at experimental forests. F.H. Eyre and W.M. Zillgitt's 1953 bulletin, [*Partial Cuttings in Northern Hardwoods of the Lake States: Twenty-Year Experimental Results*](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/156549/files/tb1076.pdf), helped establish the empirical basis for uneven-aged management and the structural targets that later appeared in marking guidance. What started as a set of treatments in particular forests eventually influenced how northern hardwood selection silviculture was taught and practiced across a much larger area.

Christel Kern and her colleagues traced that process in ["Development of the Selection System in Northern Hardwood Forests of the Lake States: An 80-Year Silviculture Research Legacy."](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/46722) Their chapter follows the work at the Dukes Experimental Forest in Michigan and the Argonne Experimental Forest in Wisconsin into marking guides, training programs, agency requirements, and everyday forestry practice. By that point, the selection system had become more than an experimental treatment. It was part of the institutional knowledge that foresters inherited, including foresters working far beyond the conditions under which the original studies were established.

Once a system becomes that familiar, the name can begin to hide as much as it explains. Laura Kenefic gets directly at that problem in ["Selective Cutting, Rehabilitation, and Alternatives for Forests of Northeastern North America and Elsewhere."](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/46376) Selective cutting and the selection method are often spoken about as though they mean the same thing, even though one may describe the removal of desirable trees while the other describes a silvicultural system intended to maintain an uneven-aged stand over repeated entries. If two managers both tell us they use "selection," we still need to know what they marked, what they retained, which regeneration conditions they were trying to create, and whether anyone returned to see what happened.

Sarita Bassil, Ralph Nyland, Christel Kern, and Laura Kenefic were able to examine some of those differences through long-term data from New York, Michigan, and Wisconsin. In ["Dynamics of the Diameter Distribution after Selection Cutting in Uneven- and Even-Aged Northern Hardwood Stands,"](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/59390) they found that repeated single-tree selection maintained relatively stable diameter distributions in the uneven-aged stands they studied. Selection-like cutting in even-aged second-growth stands followed another trajectory and moved those stands away from the recommended structural target. Similar language had been applied to treatments that were doing different things over time.

By the middle of the 1980s, researchers were also trying to describe management across a broader portion of the northern hardwood range. One of the closest historical reference points I have found is Ralph Nyland's 1986 chapter, "Important Trends and Regional Differences in Silvicultural Practice for Northern Hardwoods," published in *The Northern Hardwood Resource: Management and Potential*. Nyland also edited *Managing Northern Hardwoods*, the proceedings of a 1986 silvicultural symposium. Both works come from a period when researchers were attempting to pull together a broader picture of northern hardwood practice, even though their information had been collected from a previous generation of foresters and before computers were broadly used for data management and analysis.

The Forest Service continued the discussion through [*New Perspectives on Silvicultural Management of Northern Hardwoods*](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/4175), the proceedings of a 1988 symposium organized around the conflicting consequences of practicing northern hardwood silviculture. The contributors were already trying to think across economics, forest health, soils, nutrients, insects, diseases, and silvicultural systems. The proceedings were not a standardized census of what managers were doing across the range, but they preserved what researchers and foresters of that period thought the major choices and tradeoffs were.

The compiled [*Northern Hardwood Notes*](https://www.nrs.fs.usda.gov/pubs/nh/notes/nhnotes.pdf), including the 1992 note "Lake States Management Differs from Northeast," made a related problem explicit in plain language. Northern hardwoods may be discussed as one forest type, but a treatment developed in one part of the range may fail somewhere else. Climate, soils, species composition, competing vegetation, deer pressure, disturbance history, and regeneration conditions all affect what a treatment can accomplish. William Leak, Mariko Yamasaki, and Robbo Holleran's 2014 [*Silvicultural Guide for Northern Hardwoods in the Northeast*](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/45874) develops that regional decision process more fully by connecting landowner objectives and stand conditions with field-oriented prescriptions, including considerations such as access, regeneration, wildlife habitat, species composition, and mixedwood conditions.

Those guidebooks and proceedings explain what foresters were being advised to do. Ecological studies explain why following a treatment label or structural target may still produce an unexpected forest.

Mike Walters, Evan Farinosi, John Willis, and Kurt Gottschalk examined harvest gap size alongside competing vegetation and deer browsing in ["Managing for Diversity: Harvest Gap Size Drives Complex Light, Vegetation, and Deer Herbivory Impacts on Tree Seedlings."](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/53625) Their experiment included 18 tree species and gaps ranging from unharvested conditions to larger patch cuts. Larger gaps created more light and could support faster growth under some conditions, but shrub and herb competition increased with gap size, while deer changed which species were able to take advantage of the additional light. A manager could create the gap described in a prescription and still get a different regeneration response than expected because the gap was only one part of what the seedlings encountered.

Christel Kern's work with Stefan Hupperts, Yvette Dickinson, and Christopher Webster approaches that same problem through the range of disturbances being created by management. Their paper, ["Promoting Structural and Species Diversity in Great Lakes Northern Hardwoods,"](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/57085) considers disturbance patch size, the proportion of a stand disturbed, treatment frequency, mineral-soil exposure, and coarse woody debris. They argue that the repeated use of relatively uniform, low-severity treatments has helped homogenize managed northern hardwoods and that foresters may need a wider set of silvicultural systems, including larger patches and more variation in disturbance severity.

That gives us a much more complicated picture than a contest over which treatment is correct. Walters shows how regeneration responses depend on gap size, vegetation, and deer. Kern and her colleagues explain how repeated disturbance patterns affect diversity across stands. Kenefic's work separates selection silviculture from cutting that borrows its name without carrying through the same tending, structural, or long-term objectives. The common thread is that the prescription cannot be understood apart from the conditions, intentions, and decisions surrounding its implementation.

## From regional concerns to management on the ground

Some of the closest predecessors to our State of Management work came from regional policy and landownership assessments rather than silviculture experiments. Stephen Harper, Laura Falk, and Edward Rankin's 1990 *Northern Forest Lands Study of New England and New York* examined changing landownership and land use across Maine, New Hampshire, Vermont, and New York. It treated ownership change, forest-dependent communities, stewardship, and the regional economy as connected parts of the same problem.

The Northern Forest Lands Council extended that work in its 1994 report, [*Finding Common Ground: Conserving the Northern Forest*](https://digitalmaine.com/nflc_reports/2/). The council gathered public and institutional perspectives and translated them into recommendations concerning landownership, conservation, stewardship, and rural economies. Malmsheimer, Bentley, and Floyd then asked how much of that policy agenda had been implemented in their 2002 paper, ["Conserving the Northern Forest: A Report Card on State and Federal Implementation of Recommendations of the Northern Forest Lands Council."](https://academic.oup.com/jof/article/100/3/34/4613003) Their study followed recommendations through state and federal responses, which is close to the sequence we are interested in at another scale: what was proposed, what was adopted, what was funded, what changed, and what was documented afterward.

These studies covered four states and concentrated on policy, ownership, and institutional outcomes. They did not attempt to connect those decisions with comparable stand-level outcomes across the full northern hardwood region, but they did demonstrate how a regional assessment could work across political boundaries. Most later social-science studies of forest management returned to individual states, ownership groups, or particular programs, partly because agencies, management plans, assistance programs, and reporting systems are generally organized that way.

The ecological resource does not divide itself according to those systems. We are left with many locally useful studies that do not line up cleanly when we try to understand what is happening from Minnesota to New Brunswick.

Nan Pond, Robert Froese, and Linda Nagel came closer to the implemented-practice side of the question in ["Sustainability of the Selection System in Northern Hardwood Forests."](https://academic.oup.com/forestscience/article-abstract/60/2/374/4583770) They reconstructed conditions in 96 recently harvested Great Lakes stands under corporate, nonindustrial private, and state ownership. Only 23 percent of the harvests conformed to the accepted selection-system standards used in their study, while 41 percent were classified as cut heavily and 36 percent as cut lightly.

The percentages tell us that the accepted system and the observed harvests often differed, but they cannot fully explain how each forester reached the decision. A manager may have misunderstood a standard, deliberately chosen another approach, responded to a market or operational constraint, inherited a difficult stand, or been working toward an objective that the standard did not address. Pond and colleagues measured the result in the woods. The decision process behind that result requires interviews, plans, records, or some combination of them.

Alexander Helman and his collaborators examined part of that decision process through family forest owners in Michigan's Upper Peninsula. Their 2021 paper, ["Preferences for Northern Hardwood Silviculture among Family Forest Owners in Michigan's Upper Peninsula,"](https://doi.org/10.1093/jofore/fvaa051) compared owners' reported likelihood of using single-tree selection, shelterwood, and clearcutting. Previous experience and perceived financial benefits influenced treatment preferences, while professional forestry assistance changed the likelihood of choosing particular treatments. Their later conference paper, ["Managing for Northern Hardwoods at the Landscape Scale,"](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/66264) asks what those individual preferences add up to when many ownerships are considered across a larger area.

The Great Lakes Silviculture Library addresses another weakness in the published record: foresters learn from treatments that may never become journal articles. Moser, Sagor, Russell, and Windmuller-Campione studied the library as a common platform in ["The Great Lakes Silviculture Library: Insights into a Case Study Platform."](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/the-great-lakes-silviculture-library-insights-into-a-case-study-p/) Foresters from Minnesota, Wisconsin, and Ontario valued having cases they could understand, apply, and share across administrative boundaries. They also identified limited time as a major barrier to reading and contributing cases. Sagor and Windmuller-Campione later described this work in ["Northern Hardwoods through the Eyes of Practitioners."](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/66265)

That time constraint is easy to recognize from our interviews. Foresters hold a great deal of knowledge about treatments, adaptations, mistakes, and outcomes, but the amount that enters a shared record depends on whether their organizations give them the time and structure to document it.

The [*Proceedings of the First Biennial Northern Hardwood Conference 2021: Bridging Science and Management for the Future*](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/66254), edited by Christel Kern and Yvette Dickinson and published in 2023, brought many of these forms of evidence together. The volume includes ecological experiments, landowner preferences, practitioner cases, and work on the forestry profession. The individual contributions still use different methods and scales, but the proceedings show how far the conversation has moved from treating northern hardwood resilience as an ecological problem that can be separated from ownership, organizations, and human decision-making.

Recent studies in Québec and Ontario move the relationship between research and implementation down to individual marking decisions. Moreau and colleagues compared marking regimes across 14 managed stands spanning the northern hardwood zone of Québec in ["Simplified Tree Marking Guidelines Enhance Value Recovery as well as Stand Vigour in Northern Hardwood Forests under Selection Management."](https://academic.oup.com/forestry/article/97/2/183/7287277) They used long-term provincial trial data to test whether simpler defect classifications and more attention to crown dieback could improve residual stand vigour while recovering more value during the current harvest.

Gorgolewski and colleagues then tested a related prescription under operational conditions in Ontario in ["Implementation of an Updated Tree Marking Prescription for Selection-Managed Northern Hardwood Forests."](https://doi.org/10.5558/tfc-2025-0009) Their field trial compared the new prescription with two approaches already used in Ontario and examined which high-quality salvage trees were marked, which vigorous trees remained, and how much value was recovered. The movement between these two studies is useful for our purposes: tree-level analysis informed simulated marking, which then informed a field trial where actual operational constraints entered the process.

Nicole Rogers, Anthony D'Amato, Christel Kern, and Steve Bédard take the broadest recent geographical view in ["Northern Hardwood Silviculture at a Crossroads: Sustaining a Valuable Resource under Future Change."](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/64342) They combined inventory data from the United States and Canada with decades of scientific literature. Their analysis found some common structural conditions across mature northern hardwood forests, but regeneration, ownership, disturbance history, and species composition varied across the range. That work gives NHR a cross-border ecological starting point while also reinforcing the need to reconsider historically applied silvicultural systems under current forest conditions.

It does not claim to be a standardized assessment of how managers make and implement decisions across the whole region. From what I have been able to find so far, no recent study follows management intentions into implemented operations and then into observed outcomes under one common method across states, provinces, and ownership types. That is the specific gap I mean when I talk about the lack of a region-wide management assessment for at least 40 years. It is a narrower claim than saying that no broad northern hardwood research has been completed during that period, since Rogers and colleagues clearly provide a range-wide ecological synthesis.

## What we can honestly claim to see

Documentation shapes the management record before researchers ever begin analyzing it. You can see that pretty starkly when comparing communication with an agency forester, whether that person works for a state or the U.S. Forest Service, and communication with someone working in procurement or a more industrially driven space. The type and level of documentation can look vastly different from one firm or organization to another.

That does not establish a correct amount of reporting, data collection, documentation, or public consultation that every organization should follow. It does mean that we have a lot of layers to work through before evaluating how a manager arrived at a decision. An organization influences what information a forester collects, which decisions require approval, what must appear in a plan, which outcomes are monitored, and how much room the forester has to modify a prescription during implementation. What meets a forester's demand is very subject to whatever organization they are a part of.

Academic researchers can more easily study the parts of management that leave a strong documentary record. Informal experience, adjustments made during marking or harvesting, and knowledge retained within a procurement team or field office may be harder to recover. A difference in documentation may reflect a difference in management systems, but it can also determine which kinds of forestry become visible enough to describe in academic work.

The earlier management literature I have read tends to focus more heavily on summaries and breakdowns of managers or practices than on the decision-making processes people employ. Our interview manual attempts to get further into the depths of what management can really feel like for a forester on a unit. We ask how objectives are developed, how prescriptions change, which constraints enter the process, what happens during implementation, and how managers interpret the result afterward.

Are we perfectly capturing what people do in the woods? Only somewhat. I may speak with only a handful of people in a state, and I am not going to become an expert in forestry for that state as a result. We will never have an entirely clairvoyant view of what forestry looks like across every ownership and organization. No researcher can be omnipotent about that, and it would be foolish to try. It may already be a little foolish to cover as many states and provinces as we are attempting to cover.

That limitation has to remain visible in the final work. We are looking for attitudes, constraints, decision-making processes, and approaches that cross political boundaries, while recognizing that the people we interview cannot represent every forester working within those boundaries. We went as far as we did in recruiting different managers because we wanted perspectives tied to different types of acreage, ownership, organizational responsibility, and decision-making authority. The final assessment will not describe every day of forestry between Minnesota and New Brunswick, but it can establish a common method for interpreting which approaches are in play, how those approaches differ, and where similar problems continue to appear.

The State of Management work is trying to connect several parts of management that earlier studies often examined separately. We are interested in what managers intend to do, how those intentions become prescriptions, what is implemented, what happens afterward, and how markets, policy, ownership, organizational capacity, and forest conditions shape the movement between those stages. A treatment name is one field within a larger account of objectives, operational choices, follow-up work, site constraints, and monitoring.

For the assessment to remain honest and scientifically rigorous, it has to highlight successes while being realistic about how much foresters can do and how much capacity they have to change practices within their organizations. A forester may understand that another treatment would better fit a stand but still face market limitations, staffing shortages, policy requirements, contractor availability, landowner expectations, or organizational norms. We cannot evaluate beneficial practice as though those conditions sit outside the decision.

I keep coming back to the idea of a touchstone document. Foresters may be able to point to the final assessment and say that a research team examined northern hardwood management across its range and pulled together the numbers behind several things people in the field had understood for years. The individual conclusions may not surprise everyone. The document would still give foresters a shared account of those conditions, including evidence they can use when explaining what they see and what their organizations have the capacity to change.

That may be what the State of Management does best. It can describe the management we see, how diverse it is, and how that diversity might explain some of the forest conditions appearing across the northern hardwood range. Keeping it rigorous requires us to justify the balance across ownerships, organizations, jurisdictions, and viewpoints while being direct about what the study can and cannot represent.

We also have access to more inventory and analysis data than many of the earlier regional projects, along with the ability to connect that information with conversations about what forestry looks like on the ground. The State of Management work is being developed alongside a resource assessment, modeling, policy write-ups, and public outreach materials. When those analyses align, they can give us a more cohesive account of what northern hardwood management looks like, what has contributed to current conditions, and which directions foresters think are workable from here.

Academic literature remains the basis for all of that work. It tells us how selection systems became embedded, why similar treatment names can hide different practices, how policy recommendations move unevenly into implementation, and why regeneration outcomes depend on conditions beyond the prescription. It also tells us which links have rarely been studied together.

In its heart of hearts, this post is about where the State of Management work began. We might be way more data-rich now than we have ever been, but data alone will not tell us what beneficial practice can look and feel like in 2026. We have to combine the older scientific record with what foresters are experiencing now, assemble that information under a common method, and produce something genuinely useful for everyone and scientifically rigorous at the same time. To pull together all these different disciplines, we first have to understand where each of them started.
