Okay, the title is kind of like a Paul Revere thing, and maybe it is a little dramatic, but it fits what I heard from forest managers across the northern hardwood range. Most managers seem to know that, if they do not currently have a forest pest, pathogen, or major invasive species concern, one is likely going to be on the way pretty soon. There is an almost universal concern about which species will be affected next, what stage each problem has reached, and what managers will be able to do when it arrives. Everywhere in the northern hardwood range seems to be at a different stage of bracing for one of these problems, actively dealing with it, or trying to manage the stand that remains afterward.

What struck me initially was how much concern some managers expressed about the issues approaching their forests, and then, upon reflection, it became apparent that the design of the Northern Hardwoods mega trip had allowed me to see those concerns geographically. I have to give myself a little bit of credit for that, because I was making this kind of sweeping motion across the whole northern hardwood belt, physically moving into and out of territories where managers faced different regimes of problems. A series of disjunct Zoom interviews might have given me many of the same individual accounts, but traveling across the range allowed me to experience the transitions between them. I could leave an area where a pest had already changed management and enter one where people were still anticipating it, or where that particular issue was barely part of the conversation because foresters were more concerned about establishing the next cohort of trees.

You would be having these conversations and realize that you had actively moved into or out of an area where the management concerns were different, even though the forests were still part of the same larger northern hardwood region. The problems appeared in different pockmarks throughout the range, without a clean gradient or a universal amount of severity that any one forest would experience. Every stand is different, and every manager has a particular set of issues that they either have to attack or play defense against. Some of those issues have a lot of salience because managers are already making decisions around them, while another problem might remain out of mind until it moves closer or begins affecting the species they work with most often.

Beech bark disease is one example of how the same problem can occupy several stages at once. It has been around in New Brunswick and Maine for decades and decades, while reaching stands near the far western end of beech's range much more recently. In the places that have dealt with it for a long time, managers talk about beech thickets, beech overgrowth, and really dense beech problems associated with the disease. They can also point to surviving beech that appear to have some resilience or resistance after that long exposure. Where beech bark disease is still relatively fresh, it causes mortality at a rate that makes foresters quite uncomfortable, and managers remain uncertain about how those stands are going to fare in the future. The eastern experience can help inform the western response, although the condition of each stand and its existing beech population will shape what actually happens there.

Emerald ash borer has a similar range of circumstances, with areas that have already been heavily affected and other places where it is only beginning to arrive. Ash might not be one of the most focal species for the Northern Hardwoods project, but it is extremely important in many wetland settings, where managers described stands with ash accounting for as much as 95 percent of the composition. If emerald ash borer causes mortality of 90 percent or more, there is no easy way to retain the forest that was there before it arrived. Managers may focus their operations on harvesting ash while it still has economic value, before dead and decaying trees become unmerchantable. The response can sound almost like a shrug: emerald ash borer is here, so it is time to cut the ash because there is not much that can be done to prevent the infestation from taking most of it. In regions where the insect has not had the same impact, managers are concerned about its propagation across the landscape and what its eventual arrival will mean.

Those circumstances create several distinct periods in the management of the same problem. There is the period of bracing for an invasion, the active first stages when mortality begins and operational decisions become urgent, and the longer period of dealing with a degraded or altered stand after the species has been largely removed. Managers may also find themselves trying to defend other places by sharing what they have learned or helping someone farther along the invasion front understand what is coming. The timing is different from place to place, but the general conundrum carries across the range.

And then there is American chestnut, whose disappearance or near disappearance across the southern parts of the northern hardwood range is still felt today. People retain a cultural connection to chestnut and hold on to their chestnut furniture, even though the tree itself is largely gone from the forests where it was once an important component. We cannot magically snap our fingers and put chestnut back on the landscape because it still suffers from the blight that originally removed it. Until genetically similar and blight-resistant American chestnut can be established across the landscape, what does that mean for the future of those forests? Chestnut has attracted large and visible restoration efforts, but managers do not always see a similarly clear pathway for ash, American elm after Dutch elm disease, or every species newly threatened by a pest or pathogen.

For most foresters, education seems to be the best way they currently anticipate an incoming invasion. They stay active in the forest community, continue learning, and pay attention to how other jurisdictions or other foresters have dealt with invasions, pests, and pathogens. Hopefully, they can learn from those experiences and make a good decision, or at least know their options whenever they are up against one of these things. The physical pattern I saw during the trip also becomes a professional pattern, where knowledge from a place already dealing with the later stages of a problem can move toward places that are still bracing for it.

Knowledge does not create the money required to act on every threat, though, and the cost of managing for an invasion often prohibits what active management can look like. There is not a lot of spare money sitting around to manage for every issue that might be coming, so a manager has to weigh an extremely proactive response against the need to earn money for themselves, their landowners or clients, or to remain responsible to a public need. Multiple pests, pathogens, and invasive problems occurring within the same forest can complicate the bottom line and disrupt how work would otherwise be prioritized on a regular schedule.

In a healthy northern hardwood stand, a maintenance cut or regular entry might be passed over because an invasive pest is affecting some other part of the forest or ownership. Hemlock woolly adelgid, for instance, overlaps with many regions that possess northern hardwood species, and managers may focus their limited resources on retaining hemlock where they can rather than entering a northern hardwood stand that is otherwise doing pretty all right. There is an ongoing balance between how much managers can do, what they can afford to prioritize, and how much information they have about several problems that may be occurring at the same time.

I would revise my earlier description of these conversations as melancholy. Solemn is probably a better word, because I do not think the managers were speaking with unassailable fear or sadness. Many of them approached these problems with persistence in mind and stayed fairly stalwart about trying to solve what they could. At the same time, the pipeline never turns off. Problems continue to feed in year after year, issue after issue, and species after species, and "the hits just keep coming" was a phrase that could represent what I heard in several interviews.

Some managers expressed particular concern about the people currently learning forestry or growing into the profession, since someone at the beginning of a career may experience this pipeline, or this fire hose of problems, at an even faster pace than the current generation of managers has. That concern came up four or five times in the interviews. Yet foresters also know they are not alone, and by staying better educated they can remain more responsive and nimble when something new arrives. Continuously learning and improving one's skill set may be the main thing that drives managers toward solutions for problems that are too large for any individual forester to control.

Every forest setting has a particular set of environmental factors, whether they are climatic, ecological, geographic, or otherwise, that heavily affect its composition and determine which species can really be supported in that place. Those species give a forest its sense of place, and they are what can make it feel unique and particularly of its region. When we lose species as viable candidates at the landscape scale, we lose a piece of the identity of that place as it was. Replacement is rarely simple, since the remaining species may be poorly suited to the same environmental conditions or unable to perform the same ecological, economic, and cultural roles.

Ash trees bring that problem back into the wetter portions of northern hardwood forests, where they can account for almost the entire stand and where foresters may have very few desirable candidates to replace them. If the available replacements are undesirable species or invasives, the manager does not have a quick fix or an equivalent forest waiting to take shape. Economic theories of scarcity usually point to substitute goods, meaning an alternative that can perfectly or imperfectly replace a need. Ecologically, we find a similar premise in the way species fill niches according to habitat suitability. When there is no natural substitute, we instead have to look for a replacement, and that replacement may create a forest with a different composition, different functions, and a different sense of place.

As pests and pathogens continue to remove viable candidates, foresters also run out of options for replacing stands with something equivalent that remains healthy, regenerates well, and retains commercial or management value. Of the few dozen species that might dominate or form meaningful components of northern hardwood stands, many are subject to their own list of diseases, insects, and other problems. Managers cannot assume that a stand composed of whichever three marginal or undesirable species remain will provide the same resilience as the forest that was lost.

I think it is the responsibility of the forest manager to consider the different options available for driving the stand toward its future condition. A manager can preserve its identity and apply mitigative measures when retaining that composition is especially important and the capital is available to push for a better outcome. The same manager also has to understand that deliberately guiding a stand toward a new condition may be necessary if that stand is going to remain resilient through upcoming invasions or other problems that could arrive in the near future. Foresters seem to center reluctantly on this acceptance of resilience as they recognize that the future of a forest may depend on finding its next equilibrium.

I do not know that there is a clean solution to the conundrum, and the managers I spoke with did not pretend that they could control issues of this scale. They can respond, learn from jurisdictions that have already experienced an invasion, preserve a species where the site and available resources allow it, and guide a stand toward another composition when the previous one is no longer viable. The pipeline never turns off and the hits keep coming, which is why solemn still feels like the right word for the work of trying to retain what can be retained, learning from the places that have already been hit, and making the best decision available for the forest that will actually be there.
