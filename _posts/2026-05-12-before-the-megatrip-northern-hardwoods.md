This is the first post in what I hope becomes a longer record of field notes, road updates, and conversations from the woods. I have started calling this upcoming stretch of fieldwork the “mega trip,” mostly because it has turned into a six-week loop across nearly the whole project area. I will be driving through the north-central and northeastern parts of the northern hardwood range to visit the forests, managers, agencies, landowners, and organizations that shape how these woods are cared for.

Before I leave, I want to lay out what this project is, what I mean by the Northern Hardwood resource, and why I am spending this much time talking with the people who manage it.

Northern hardwood forests are one of the main forest types across the Lake States, the Northeast, and parts of eastern Canada. For this project, we are using the term for forests built around maple, birch, and beech, with basswood, black cherry, and sometimes oak mixed in depending on place. Many people know these forests even if they do not use the label every day. They are the sugar maple stands tapped for maple syrup, the mixed-species slopes of the Adirondacks, the county and state forests of the northern Lake States, the ridges and valleys of northern Pennsylvania and New Jersey, and the managed private and Crown lands of Ontario, Québec, and New Brunswick, where sugar maple is proudly featured as a national symbol on pretty much anything related to Canada.

They are also working forests, community forests, wildlife habitat, recreation ground, cultural landscapes, conservation land, and long-term investments. That is part of why they are difficult to talk about as a single thing. A northern hardwood stand can be managed for timber, habitat, maple syrup, water, recreation, carbon, family ownership goals, or some negotiated version of all of those. What happens depends on who owns the land, what policies apply, which markets are nearby, which species are regenerating, how heavy deer browse is, what past management left behind, and what kind of future the manager is trying to build toward.

The Northern Hardwoods Resilience Project is trying to understand those differences across the range. We are trying to learn what is working, what is getting in the way, and where managers see gaps between silvicultural intent, implementation, and long-term forest outcomes. Northern hardwood silviculture has a long literature behind it. This project adds another source of information: the experience of people making decisions in stands now, under current ecological, economic, and institutional constraints.

That is where this trip comes in.

The “mega trip” will take me through 14 of the 15 political units covered by the project. That means all 12 U.S. states in the project area, plus New Brunswick and Ontario. I will return to Québec later for a separate set of conversations. My goal for this first loop is simple: go to the places where these forests are being managed, talk with the people doing the work, and listen carefully.

The interviews focus on managers’ experiences with northern hardwoods. That can mean a lot of things: regeneration problems, practices that seem to be working, treatments that are much harder to carry out than they look on paper, and the tradeoffs that come up when people manage for timber, wildlife, climate resilience, recreation, conservation, cultural goals, or some combination of those. A large part of this project is about how human goals and decision-making meet the forest itself. Silviculture does not happen in a vacuum. It happens through people, institutions, policies, markets, equipment, access, weather, budgets, values, and landowner objectives.

We are also trying to sample management perspectives across the ownership and management settings that make up the Northern Hardwood resource. For the interviews, we are sorting managers into five broad groups: national-level managers, state/provincial/local managers, non-governmental organizations, private landowners and companies, and Tribal or First Nations managers.

We separated those groups because they operate under different rules. In the United States, national-level management includes national forests and other federally managed lands. State and local management includes state forests, state DNR lands, wildlife areas, municipal forests, and county forests, especially in parts of the Lake States where county forests are a large part of the land base. On the Canadian side, Ontario, Québec, and New Brunswick all have large areas of Crown land, which are held publicly and managed at the provincial level. NGOs, including land trusts and organizations such as The Nature Conservancy and the Appalachian Mountain Club, often manage land with strong conservation goals. Private ownership is the largest and most varied piece of the northern hardwood land base; it includes family forest landowners, consulting foresters working with those landowners, hunting clubs, larger industrial owners, timber investment management organizations, and real estate investment trusts. Tribal and First Nations managers have another set of governance structures, responsibilities, values, and long-term relationships with forested land.

The categories are rough, but the differences behind them matter. Legal standards, institutional responsibilities, management goals, and day-to-day constraints change across ownerships. A state forester, a consulting forester, an industrial land manager, a land trust manager, and a Tribal forestry department may all be working in northern hardwoods. The choices available to each of them can still look very different. That is one reason we should expect different approaches, different rule-making frameworks, and different outcomes across the same general forest type.

Each state or province also has its own mix. Maine, for example, has a large amount of NGO ownership relative to many other parts of the region, with organizations such as the Appalachian Mountain Club and The Nature Conservancy managing substantial forest holdings. By comparison, in Québec, NGO ownership makes up a much smaller share of the Northern Hardwood land base. Some jurisdictions have public land systems that shape a large share of management. Others are dominated by private ownership. New Jersey has a majority share of state-owned and managed hardwoods, even though it is a small and more densely populated state. Some states have strong county forest systems. Others have more fragmented family forest ownership. The project is designed to pay attention to those differences instead of treating the whole Northern Hardwood belt as one uniform region.

The interview sampling is built around that logic. Across the full project, we aim to conduct more than 100 interviews with northern hardwood managers. Within each political unit, we want the sample to reflect the management context of that jurisdiction as closely as it can. If a large share of the resource in a state or province is managed by one group, that group should show up in the conversations.

A simplified Michigan example helps. If we planned three interviews in Michigan, and the relevant northern hardwood land base were roughly one-third private landowners, one-third state forest, and one-third U.S. Forest Service, we would try to interview one person from each group. With six interviews, we could add depth within those groups: two levels of state forest management in two management units, two Forest Service perspectives split between the Upper and Lower Peninsula, and two private-land perspectives, such as a consulting forester working with family forest landowners and a representative from a larger forest-owning firm.

At the same time, the full sample also needs range-wide diversity. That means talking with consulting foresters, public land managers, procurement foresters, industrial private landowners, NGO managers, family forest landowner-facing professionals, Tribal and First Nations forestry staff, and others who can speak to different parts of the management picture.

By the end of the process, I want a clearer picture of northern hardwood management in 2026 across a broad region. That means documenting what managers are trying to accomplish, which practices they rely on, where regeneration is working or failing, how markets and policies shape decisions, and where people are changing their approach as forest conditions change.

I am especially interested in the knowledge that already moves around this region, and the knowledge that does not. A lesson from New Brunswick may be useful in Minnesota. A Wisconsin example may help frame a question in New York. A management problem in Ontario may sound familiar to foresters in Pennsylvania. Those connections do not always happen on their own. The region is large, the ecology and climate vary, and managers often work within the boundaries of their own state, province, ownership, agency, or professional network. Part of this project is carrying conversations across those boundaries.

As I travel, I will post updates along the way. I do not expect to have formal findings while I am still on the road, and I want to treat the conversations with the respect and confidentiality they deserve. There should still be plenty to share: stories from the trip, reflections from different parts of the range, photos of forests and field sites, and a few observations about how the resource changes as I move from the Lake States into the Northeast and then into eastern Canada.

I also hope to have a live map available so people can follow the journey. I want this series to show where the project is going, what kinds of forest settings I am visiting, and how broad the Northern Hardwood resource is once you start moving through it by road.

More than anything, this trip is about listening. Northern hardwoods are managed by people with different goals, constraints, experiences, and relationships to the land. To understand how this forest resource can become more resilient, we need to understand the forests themselves and the decisions being made in them every day. That is what I am heading out to learn.

I am curious to see what these forests have been telling the people who work in them.
