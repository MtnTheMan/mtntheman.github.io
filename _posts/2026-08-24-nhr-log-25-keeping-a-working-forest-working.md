On a short little road trip out from Fredericton, the provincial capital of New Brunswick, I visited Millville, which has a roadside sculpture advertised as the world's largest maple leaf. Nearby Nackawic has the world's largest axe. I wanted to get a good overview of the surrounding forest, so I stopped at a local convenience store and asked the people there where I could look out over the area. One of the locals said, "Oh, you could go up to the watchtower," as if this were a normal destination that anybody passing through would know about.

The watchtower looked similar to an old fire tower, except that it had been built in someone's backyard. There were no signs identifying it as a public commodity or public good, and there was nothing that formally invited a random traveler to climb it. It was simply common knowledge among local people that the family encouraged visitors to use their lookout tower. The family owned the property and a lot of the surrounding forest, and they had built the tower partly because they wanted to give something back, although they also got to stand up there and look over all their stuff.

I drove my silly little sedan, a rental car that probably should not have been on that road, right up to the base of the tower. I hung out there for maybe thirty seconds or a minute before, out of the blue, a local guy showed up and struck up a conversation with me as we climbed. We talked about the family that had built the tower, the surrounding woods, and how connected people in that part of New Brunswick were to forestry and the timber economy. It was a very blue-collar-oriented part of the province, and it seemed as though nearly everyone was connected to the timber industry in some way or form.

From the top, we could hear timber harvesting equipment operating maybe half a mile, or about a kilometer, away. It was a Sunday, as far as I remember, and the equipment was running somewhere beyond the tower as we looked across this patchwork mosaic of trees. The entire view had the feeling of a working landscape that people were actively using, harvesting, and making their livings from. The machinery did not seem like something that had to be apologized for or hidden; it was part of what people did there.

What stood out to me in Millville was the relationship of trust among local landowners, the forestry sector, and a community that understood how important that sector was. Forestry and the timber economy felt like part of the cultural identity of the place. Familiarity with timber harvesting came with the territory, and that familiarity appeared to shape how people understood the sound of harvesting equipment, the sight of a recent entry, or a landowner making decisions about the surrounding forest.

Standing on the tower, I found myself wondering whether people in a place like Millville have more acceptance for the background noise of harvesting equipment, or more trust in the institutional wisdom of a forester, logger, or landowner who wants to manage resources as they see fit. That kind of trust does not develop without a working relationship among the people who own forests, the people who manage them, and the communities that depend on them.

I had already been thinking about [variability in planning documentation](https://www.mtntheman.com/northern-hardwoods/research/travel/2026/08/21/nhr-log-24-variability-in-planning-documentation.html), which was the subject of the last post, but the trip kept putting a related kind of variability in front of me. People have different levels of familiarity with what active forest management really looks like, and the firms and agencies responsible for managing forest land have different levels of responsibility to those people. Both of those conditions shift across the northern hardwood belt.

The contrast was especially apparent when I talked with managers in more densely populated parts of New York, Connecticut, Rhode Island, and Massachusetts. Alongside the ecological and legal questions associated with a harvest, those managers were thinking about how their work would look to the outside world and what repercussions might follow if something went wrong or simply looked poor. There was a recurring impression that forestry could be perceived negatively by people who were unfamiliar with responsible forest management. Putting any chainsaw to a tree can be a hard sell when someone begins from the assumption that leaving every tree alone is the healthiest possible outcome.

I also heard an underlying disgruntlement from some managers toward city residents whose opinions they might have to respond to. Some felt that the public did not fully appreciate what timber harvesting contributes to local economies or how much ecological science goes into planning a sustainable forest entry. I would be careful about turning this into a clean rural-urban split, since rural residents are not automatically informed about forestry and urban residents are not automatically opposed to it. Still, part of my interpretation is that the long movement of the American population away from a largely agricultural society and toward more urban residency has left many people physically separated from working forests and the decisions involved in managing them.

Foresters, at their heart of hearts, have to believe in both conservation and utilitarianism. Those ideas can appear conflicting to someone without training in forestry, although much of a forester's job involves finding a workable balance between them. The public may picture a no-harvest scenario as the healthiest thing that can happen to a forest, when in actuality harvesting can either be detrimental or provide considerably more benefit over the long run than simply leaving the forest alone.

Many northern hardwood stands have been influenced by high grading and other practices that removed desirable trees without creating the composition or structure needed for future resilience. Some stands may be overstocked or increasingly susceptible to disease and disturbance, and an active entry may improve growing conditions, encourage a more diverse or resilient cohort, or move the stand toward a better condition. I think Ethan Tapper does a good job of explaining this dichotomy in *How to Love a Forest*: management can harm a forest, and positively oriented management can also help it.

Foresters have to bridge the divide between what the public expects a healthy forest to look like and what good management could accomplish on a particular site at a particular time. In places where people already understand what a working forest looks and sounds like, the divide may be fairly narrow. In places where most people encounter forestry through a visible harvest along a road, a public meeting, or a controversy after the work has begun, managers have much more explaining to do. Keeping a working forest working can benefit both people and the forest in the long run, but the profession has to communicate how and why.

The responsibility to communicate also changes according to who is doing the management and what that actor receives from the public. A public agency such as the U.S. Forest Service must be directly responsive to the public, including anyone who lives in a city all the way to people living in rural communities that are more directly associated with national forests. Formal review and public-comment processes are built into federal land management, including the processes associated with the National Environmental Policy Act. Public accountability is part of the structure through which those lands are managed.

Private ownership can carry a different level of responsibility, although I think that depends partly on what the landowner gets out of the public benefit. If a private landowner receives tax benefits for forest management, the underlying idea is that the landowner is producing enough of a public good to justify that benefit. Some amount of reporting responsibility should probably follow from that exchange, or at least some responsiveness to whatever landscape-level plan may exist. I am not saying that this reporting framework is consistently present or that every tax program currently requires it. I am saying that receiving a public benefit carries an assumed responsibility to provide some public good in return.

A private landowner who receives no tax benefit, grant, or other public resource may have less formal responsibility under that framework. Yet the effects of forest management do not stop at the property boundary. Large timber investment companies affect local communities, uphold or weaken local economies, and influence whether forestry produces a net positive externality for the surrounding area. Those effects provide a reason for responsible management, public reporting, and some degree of community acceptance even where the law requires less participation than it would from a public agency.

An example I kept thinking about, even though it sits outside the northern hardwood belt, is the way some large timber companies in the Pacific Northwest provide recreational opportunities on private industrial land. They have authority over the parcel, but they still want buy-in from local communities and even from people traveling through the area. Markets, community relationships, and the continuing acceptance of forestry all give a private company reasons to care about public perception.

Crown-land licensees in New Brunswick introduce another arrangement. A private company operates on public land through a 25-year forest management agreement with the province, with management and operating plans, reporting requirements, and periodic provincial review. The company is responsible to a public agency, which is then responsible to the public. From the conversations I had, I came away with the impression that this middleman arrangement can make public accountability feel less immediate even though formal provincial oversight remains. A licensee may have considerable flexibility to conduct business over a long period without carrying the direct public-facing responsibility that I would expect from an agency managing the land itself.

I keep coming back to the idea that these responsibilities fall along a spectrum and carry different weights. Who owns the land is one part of it, but so are the public resources an actor receives, the agency or constituency to which that actor reports, and the effects that management has on surrounding communities. Those responsibilities could be mapped onto an overall planning or reporting strategy, although the appropriate level of accountability would look different for each actor.

It is hard to make the argument that members of the public need to do more on behalf of understanding foresters. A little more open-mindedness and a little less NIMBYism around forestry would help, but all in all, foresters can do a better job of communicating. We repeatedly talk about how there are too few people communicating forestry in a public forum, and that has been a very recurrent theme in this research.

I do not know exactly where that role should sit. We could lean more on organizations such as the Forest Stewards Guild or the Society of American Foresters to talk about forestry with the public, although no single organization will be the silver bullet for enabling good forestry across the board. Individual foresters and landowners also have some responsibility to explain what they are doing before a harvest becomes a source of conflict.

Forestry is an expert field with its own research base, professional experience, and continuing debate. Scrutiny can be healthy in establishing what we consider responsible practice, but debate among experts usually begins with a common baseline acceptance of knowledge. My concern is that forestry can find itself subject to nonexperts contributing opinions that affect what can and cannot happen on the ground without accounting for the ecological science behind a prescription.

The analogy I kept reaching for was food. People may debate dietary guidance or which chemicals should be allowed in food, but we still expect food and health experts to contribute specialized knowledge to those decisions. If someone says that you should eat your vegetables, most people do not demand a public referendum on whether vegetables are healthy before accepting the recommendation. Forestry can end up debating its baseline knowledge before it ever reaches the specific question of how trees will grow in a particular setting over a particular period of time.

Public involvement should remain part of decisions affecting public land and public resources, while technical opinions should receive weight according to the knowledge behind them. An assumption that every harvest is inherently harmful can prevent the kinds of entries that might improve forest conditions over time. Foresters have to explain that distinction more clearly than they often have, especially in places where people no longer encounter working forests as part of ordinary life.

In Millville, trust had grown through repeated experience with forestry, the timber economy, and the local landowners who were part of both. A tower in somebody's backyard functioned as a public lookout without a sign, and the sound of harvesting equipment on a Sunday blended into the background of the place. Where that daily familiarity is absent, foresters and forestry organizations will have to build trust more deliberately. I came away thinking that a working forest can remain working only if foresters are willing to explain what they are doing, what they expect the forest to look like afterward, and what responsibility they carry if the entry does not produce the outcome they promised.
