Northern Hardwoods Resilience Project

For the Northern Hardwoods Resilience project, I examine how the U.S. Forest Service and state partners use BMP guides and management objectives to plan and evaluate silvicultural treatments in hardwood forests, including treatments intended to meet oak–hickory stand goals. I compare guidance, prescriptions, and post-treatment assessments to see how written plans translate into sustained yield, habitat, and local economic outcomes. I also study how cost-share programs and technical assistance shape decisions on private land.

Agroforestry in Lake States

This work grew out of my master’s thesis on the spatial patterns and human decisions behind agroforestry adoption in the Lake States, available here: Read the thesis. I am continuing the research by studying how practice standards, technical assistance, and advice from partner organizations reach landowners and affect what happens on the ground. I am also interested in how experience from completed projects feeds back into later guidance.

Predicting Windbreaks or Riparian Forest Buffers with Random Forest Models

For my GEO 866 final project, I tested whether Random Forest models could predict where linear small woody features such as windbreaks, hedgerows, and riparian buffers occur across Michigan, Wisconsin, and Ohio. I combined a mapped dataset of woody features from 35 study counties with wind speed, soil erodibility, land cover, crop type, and impervious-surface data. I then compared point-based, log-transformed, and one-kilometer raster models. The raster model did the best job of reproducing broad spatial patterns, although it overpredicted urban areas and left clustered residuals. The project gave me practical experience with spatial statistics, raster processing, machine learning, and mapping in R.

Synthesis of Citizen Science on Public Lands

I am comparing where citizen-science observations occur on public and private land, beginning with iNaturalist records joined to the Protected Areas Database of the United States. The analysis measures differences in observation density, taxonomic coverage, and verification across ownership classes. I am also looking at the agency choices behind those patterns, including geoprivacy rules, bioblitzes, interpretive signs, and partnerships with local groups, and whether those choices make the data more useful for planning and management.

Land Cover in Michigan

I am producing a statewide, 0.6-meter land-cover and canopy map for Michigan from 2022 NAIP imagery using a U-Net workflow. The project is meant to support precision-conservation work by agencies and other public users. That includes planning, monitoring, and policy evaluation. The data and code will be open.

Roads and their Genesis/Utilization on USFS Lands

I am studying how timber roads on U.S. Forest Service lands are planned, built, maintained, and handled after a harvest. The work follows both the written standards and the decisions made over a road’s useful life, including whether it remains open, is repurposed, or is decommissioned. I am especially interested in how engineering requirements and environmental rules affect public access, local jobs, maintenance costs, and the choices available for later management.

Woody Riparian Buffer Index and Functional Forest Class

I developed the Woody Riparian Buffer Index to measure how much woody vegetation protects streams and lakes, using both vector and raster methods with distance-decay scoring on each bank. The index produces watershed summaries and maps of buffer gaps that can guide restoration work. I am also testing whether sub-meter land-cover data detect narrow buffers and edges more accurately than coarser products.

iNaturalist Administration + Guidance

While working for the National Park Service, I built and administered iNaturalist projects across the agency. The goal was straightforward: give parks a consistent way to organize observations while making it easier for visitors and staff to contribute. I still volunteer on this work by maintaining projects and improving guidance on geoprivacy, sensitive species, bioblitzes, and record verification. See the nationwide National Park Service project on iNaturalist for more detail.

Predicting Invasive Species on Public Lands with Citizen Science

I use iNaturalist observations to estimate which invasive plants may be recorded next on public lands. This is a watch-list method, not a species-distribution model. For each park or managed area, I compare the plants recorded inside its boundary with nearby records, rank the 100 most common nonnative plants within 160 kilometers, and narrow the list using habitat suitability and state priorities. I first developed the method for my undergraduate honors thesis on early detection in the National Park Service, and it later contributed to a peer-reviewed study of 36 lower-budget NPS units. Managers can rerun the analysis as new observations come in.

Responsible Stewardship and Access

Responsible stewardship is a thread that runs through my work rather than a single project. I am interested in how agencies balance public access with safety, maintenance capacity, and ecological responsibility, and how standards and professional judgment shape those decisions. Across projects, I return to the same practical questions: who benefits from access, who pays for upkeep, and what agencies can realistically deliver with the evidence and resources they have.

Relict Gullies and Thermo-Erosion in Northern Lower Michigan
  • In a graduate seminar, I helped map and describe thousands of shallow, relict gullies cut into sandy upland ridges in northern Lower Michigan. We used high-resolution terrain data to delineate the gully networks and their contributing basins, then tested whether so many downslope channels could have formed in highly permeable sand without frozen ground. Our results suggest that ice-rich permafrost promoted runoff and thermo-erosion during the Late Pleistocene. The published paper documents the gully forms and spatial patterns and describes a method that can be applied to other cold-region settings: Read the paper.
Mountain Pine Beetle Agent-Based Model
  • For my GEO 869 final project at MSU, I built an agent-based model of mountain pine beetle spread and tree mortality. I used it to test how assumptions about beetle life cycles and tree death change the simulated outbreak, and to connect those ecological processes with management decisions.
