# Deer research in the Northern Hardwoods Pressure Atlas

*Status: September 25, 2026.* The atlas is testing how several imperfect signals can help describe regional variation in white-tailed deer abundance. Its currently published interactive product is the [experimental T21 camera–GBIF map](/assets/maps/northern-hardwood-deer-t21-relative-index-experimental/). That map displays modeled, relative observation signals over an aligned 5-km reporting grid. It is not a deer count, population density estimate, browse-impact map, or validated management ranking. Its [methods page](/northern-hardwood-deer-t21-relative-index-experimental-methods/) documents sources, years, support gaps, sensitivity, and the limits of geographic transfer.

## Why examine a third source?

Camera encounters, opportunistic wildlife reports, and agency harvest records observe deer through different processes. Cameras provide a structured encounter signal at relatively few deployments. GBIF records add broad geographic context but depend heavily on where and what people report. Harvest records can add information from places with little camera or GBIF support, but they also depend on hunter access, regulations, participation, reporting, and the management boundaries used by each state. None is an interchangeable measure of animal density.

The private harvest-inclusive research therefore keeps the three components visible and distinct. Harvest is treated at the agency's native reporting support before comparison on the atlas grid; assigning a unit's relative value to intersecting cells does not create cell-level harvest observations. Within-state comparisons help avoid treating differently reported state totals as directly comparable, but they do not correct unknown within-state hunting effort or huntability. Camera evidence is given the strongest interpretive role, with harvest and GBIF contributing separately rather than simply replacing one another. Alternative combinations and source disagreements remain essential sensitivity checks.

This is **not** the joint statistical likelihood used by [Gilbert and colleagues' integrated harvest-and-camera species-distribution study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biocon.2021.109147). That study is an important methodological precedent for combining observation processes, not evidence that the atlas's present weighted research index estimates absolute abundance. The current camera evidence comes from [SNAPSHOT USA 2019–2023](https://doi.org/10.5061/dryad.k0p2ngfhn) and [2024](https://doi.org/10.5061/dryad.bnzs7h4qf); the published GBIF component uses the cited [white-tailed deer](https://doi.org/10.15468/dl.djeu57) and [Mammalia](https://doi.org/10.15468/dl.sv79qv) downloads. The T21 methods page explains their versions and mixed record-level licenses.

## What remains private

The subsequent harvest comparison and three-source map files include source-native values, boundaries, or derivatives for which exact-edition public reuse has not been cleared. Maine source terms require particular care; making a page unindexed would not make its embedded data private. Recent work also corrected boundary-support problems in Indiana and Michigan, but that geometry correction is not independent evidence that the resulting deer-abundance pattern is biologically valid. Boundary vintages, source rights, hunter effort, and geographic-transfer performance still need review.

Accordingly, this page publishes **no** harvest layer, source-native management-unit map, state agreement statistic, unpublished grid table, or later private viewer. Those materials are retained in the project research archive, not in this website's public assets.

## Release standard

A future public harvest-inclusive map will require a version-specific source and boundary rights review, an independently built redacted package, checks that browser source and downloads contain no restricted detail, clear native-support and missingness labels, and an honest validation assessment. Until then, the published T21 map remains a candidate relative signal for inspection and hypothesis generation—not a measured deer-abundance surface.
